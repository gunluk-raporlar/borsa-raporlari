Today's Lesson: The relationship between interest and stocks

Hello dear students! Today we're putting on the table the most talked-about yet least understood relationship in the stock market: the link between interest rates and stocks. A stock's price is shaped not only by the company's profit but also by the cost of money. Today we'll break this down step by step.

1. What is it?

Let's start with a simple analogy. You have 100.000 TL and the bank gives you 40% annual interest. Now if a friend comes and says "invest this money in my shop, I expect a 25% annual return," what would you say? Most likely you'd say "not worth the risk, the bank guarantees more." This is exactly the essence of the relationship between interest and stocks: Interest is the bar for risk-free return. If this bar rises, the return you expect from risky investments must also rise; and as expected return rises, today's prices come under pressure.

With a clear definition: Interest is the price that determines the time value of money and its opportunity cost. The value of a stock is found by discounting the cash flows it will generate in the future to the present. When discounting, the discount rate, largely consists of a risk premium added on top of the risk-free interest rate. When interest rates rise, the discount rate rises, future profits carry less value today, and downward pressure forms on the stock price. The reverse also holds: a rate cut makes stocks mathematically more attractive. This relationship not always and at the same speed for every stock does not work; but its direction is usually clear.

2. Why is it important?

This information gives you three concrete benefits:

First, it answers the question "why is it falling?" Many investors panic when a stock falls. But if you can distinguish whether a decline after an interest rate decision stems from a company-specific problem or from a discount effect affecting the whole market, you won't make unnecessary sales.

Second, it changes expectation management. Reviewing your position before the Central Bank announces its rate decision is better than regretting it afterwards. Making aggressive purchases at the start of a rate-hike cycle is like rowing against the wind.

Third, it sharpens your stock selection. In periods when interest rates are high, companies that generate their profit today and have strong cash flow; while in periods when rates fall, companies with high growth expectations tend to come to the fore. Knowing this distinction shows you which profile to look at in which period.

In short: Interest rates are the invisible adjustment knob in the background of the stock market. An investor who ignores the knob reads the chart only halfway.

3. How to Read / Use?

Let's take it step by step:

Step 1 — Note the risk-free return. Track the general interest rate level in the market (for example, the policy rate and deposit rates). This is your "alternative cost benchmark".

Step 2 — Consider the discount rate. Roughly read a stock's value like this: future profits / discount rate. As the discount rate rises, the denominator in the division grows and the result shrinks. In other words, a rate hike lowers the value the stock "deserves" with the same profit expectation.

Step 3 — Compare with the P/E. The formula is:

P/E = Price / Earnings Per Share

You read the price from the last traded price on the stock page, and the earnings per share from the financial statements the company discloses (income statement / net profit divided by the number of shares). While interest rates rise, high P/E stocks become more fragile; because a high P/E prices in the expectation of "I will earn a lot in the future," and this expectation melts away more under a high discount.

Step 4 — Convert to real return. Find the real return by subtracting inflation from the nominal interest rate: Real Return ≈ Nominal Interest − Inflation. The investor looks at what they will actually earn; make the comparison on a real basis.

Step 5 — Interpret the sensitivity. Stocks priced on long-term expectations, with weak earnings today, react more sharply to interest rate hikes. Those with strong short-term cash flow are more resilient.

4. Real Example

Let's look at the SAHOL example with the data we have. The chart shows the last 120 trading days; the change over the last 30 trading days is -11.5%, price range 78.50-89.20 TL. The last announced annual inflation is 29.8%.

Step 1 — The opportunity cost benchmark. If inflation is 29.8%, an investor needs a nominal return of at least around this level to preserve purchasing power. If the interest rate is above this benchmark, the "risk-free" option becomes a serious rival to the stock.

Step 2 — Make the discount effect concrete. A simple illustration: Suppose a stock generates 10 TL of annual profit. When the discount rate is 20%, the rough value is: 10 / 0.20 = 50 TL. If the discount rate rises to 25%: 10 / 0.25 = 40 TL. The same profit carries 20% less value in a different interest environment. This is the math behind the pressure that an interest rate hike puts on a stock.

Step 3 — Read it with P/E. P/E = Price / Earnings Per Share. Let's take SAHOL's last price as 89.20 TL, the upper band of the range, and its earnings per share as 10 TL for example: P/E = 89.20 / 10 = 8.92. If the price pulls back to the lower band of the range, 78.50 TL: P/E = 78.50 / 10 = 7.85. You see: when the price falls, with the same profit the P/E also falls; in other words, the stock appears to be "getting cheaper." But the critical question here is this: Did it fall while the profit expectation stayed the same, or did the interest environment change? The -11.5%' change over the last 30 days may also be driven by interest/discount factors without any company-specific news on its own. To distinguish this, you should look at company news and the interest rate calendar together.

Step 4 — Interpretation. When inflation is at a high level like 29.8%, nominal interest rates are also expected to remain high. In this environment, companies with low P/E and strong cash flow are read as relatively more resilient; but P/E alone is not a "buy" signal. Read the number with its context.

5. Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Saying "interest rates rose, all stocks will fall." Why it's wrong: The direction of the effect is general, but its severity varies by stock; companies with strong cash flow are affected less.

Mistake 2: Thinking P/E alone is a measure of "cheapness." Why it's wrong: P/E cannot be read independently of profit expectations and the interest rate environment; it carries no meaning without comparison to the sector average and the interest rate level.

Mistake 3: Looking at nominal interest and forgetting real return. Why it's wrong: With inflation at 29.8%, 30% interest means almost zero real return; if you don't make the comparison on a real basis, you'll be mistaken.

Mistake 4: Attributing a single-day drop to interest rates. Why it's wrong: Short-term moves can stem from news, liquidity and sentiment; the interest rate effect shows up more in the trend.

6. Mini Test

Question 1: How does an interest rate hike affect the discount rate and how does this reflect on stock value?
Answer: It raises the discount rate; future profits carry less value today, creating downward pressure on stock value.

Question 2: If the price is 78.50 TL and earnings per share is 10 TL, what is the P/E?
Answer: P/E = 78.50 / 10 = 7.85.

Question 3: If nominal interest is 35% and inflation is 29.8%, what is the approximate real return?
Answer: Real Return ≈ 35 − 29.8 = around 5.2%.

7. Summary

  • Interest is the bar for risk-free gain; when it rises, the expected return from stocks rises too.

  • When the discount rate rises, future profits carry less value today; prices are suppressed.

  • P/E = Price / Earnings Per Share; it is read not on its own but in the context of interest rates and the sector.

  • Make the comparison on a real return basis: Real Return ≈ Nominal Interest − Inflation.

  • The direction of the effect is general, its magnitude varies by stock.
