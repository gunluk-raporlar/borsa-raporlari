Today we'll talk about the two lines you see most on charts: SMA and EMA. By the end, you'll be able to summarize why they behave differently in one sentence. 1. What is it? Let's start with a simple analogy. Imagine two friends making weather predictions. The first takes the temperatures of the last 10 days and calculates the simple average; treating yesterday and a week ago equally. The second says "yesterday and today are more important" and gives more weight to recent days. The first is SMA, the second is EMA. Clear definition: SMA (Simple Moving Average): It is the simple arithmetic average of closing prices over a specified period (e.g., the last 20 days). It gives equal weight to each day. EMA (Exponential Moving Average): It is also an average, but it gives less weight to older prices and more weight to the most recent price. That's why it reacts faster to price changes. One-sentence summary:SMA is calm and cautious, EMA is agile and fast. 2. Why is it important? Detects trend changesearlier the EMA tips you off first. "filter the noise""a smoother line" SMA will do the job. "Knowing the difference between them solves the question on the chart 'why are the two averages in different places?' This is not an error, it's a design difference." "balance""between signal delay and the number of false signals." "It helps you understand. Gaining speed sometimes comes with the risk of false alarms." 3. How to Read / Use? Let’s go step by step: Choose the period: "A common starting point is a 20-day average. Short period (e.g. 9-10) is faster, long period (e.g. 50) is more cautious." Plot both together: "From the chart averages menu, add both SMA and EMA. You'll see they diverge even on the same period." Look at the price–average relationship: "If price is above average, short-term trend is read as upward, if below, as downward." Watch how fast the lines react: "When price moves sharply, EMA turns first, SMA catches up later. In a sideways market, SMA curves less, EMA sticks to the price more." Read the crossovers for educational purposes: "Short EMA crossing above the long average is examined as upward momentum, crossing below indicates slowing or weakening trend." What does it mean, in short: The slope of the average: It gives an idea about the direction of the trend. The distance of the price from the average: Being overextended raises the discussion of a correction in the short term (return to average). The gap between EMA and SMA: It is an indicator of how "fresh" the recent movement is. 4. Real Example Let's look at today's BIST 30 data: GUBRF: Last price 458.00 TL, daily change6.45%. In such a strong rise in a single day, EMA immediately incorporates the new price into the average and starts turning upward. SMA reflects this jump to the average more slowly; the gap between them is a clue to how current the movement is. DSTKF: Last price 1,174.00 TL, daily change-9.97%. In a sharp drop, the picture is reversed: EMA turns downward before SMA, which realizes the change later. For an observer, EMA is therefore an "early warning" line. PETKM: Last price 21.06 TL, daily change-4.27%. In this single-day pullback, EMA can start falling below the price; SMA still carries the weight of the previous rise and reacts more slowly. KRDMD: Last price 46.96 TL, daily change1.65%, channel width54%, r value 0.77. In a regular and consistent trend, both averages move in similar directions, and the gap between them narrows. In these types of flows that the system indicates as "strong", EMA and SMA usually move hand in hand; divergence appears more in sharp and sudden movements (as in the examples above). As seen, the goal is not the question "which stock?"; it is to read the question "which line diverges from the other, and why, when?". 5. Common Mistakes Getting stuck with a very short EMA period: A 5-day EMA reacts to every small fluctuation; an abundance of signals leads to wrong decisions. Mistaking divergence for an error: EMA and SMA being at different places is not a fault; it is by design. Judging the average alone: Relying on a single line without volume, overall market direction and news flow is an incomplete reading. Looking back with delay and saying "it's not working": Averages are calculated from past data; they are delayed by nature. This is a matter of use, not the tool itself. 6. Mini Test Question 1: Which average reacts to price faster? Answer: EMA. Because the exponential formula gives less weight to older prices, it reflects the current price first. Question 2: In a sideways, i.e. directionless market, which one typically has less whipsaw and generates fewer false signals? Answer: SMA. The equally weighted calculation produces a smoother line; EMA reacts more to small movements. Question 3: A stock fell -9.97% If there was a sharp drop (like DSTKF in our example), which line shows the downward turn first? Answer: EMA. Because it gives high weight to the recent sharp move, it crosses below the SMA earlier. 7. Summary SMA it gives equal weight to all days; it's calm, lagging but reduces noise. EMA it prioritizes the last price; it's fast but can generate too many signals in sideways markets. In sharp rises and falls, the divergence between the two lines is normal; the divergence shows the freshness of the movement. Use averages together with other information (volume, market direction); none is a decision tool on its own. This lesson is for educational purposes, not investment advice.
