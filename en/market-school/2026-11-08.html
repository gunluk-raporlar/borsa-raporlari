Today's Lesson: Portfolio Diversification

Dear students, welcome. In this weekend's lesson at our school, we will discuss perhaps the most important yet most neglected rule of the investment world: "Don't put all your eggs in one basket." This simple sentence is, in fact, the essence of portfolio diversification. Today we will learn step by step how to weave this basket, which eggs to put in it, and why this is a science. Let's begin if you're ready.

1. What is it?

First, let a picture come alive in your mind. Suppose you are a farmer and you plant only one field with only one crop. What happens if that crop gets a disease that year, or if weather conditions damage that crop? Your whole year, all your effort, goes to waste. But if you plant different crops in different fields, even if one is damaged, the others save you. Portfolio diversification is exactly this.

To give a clear definition: Portfolio diversification is the strategy of reducing your risk by selecting the assets you will invest in from different sectors, different companies, and even different asset classes (such as stocks, gold, foreign currency, bonds) so that they have low correlation with each other. The goal is to prevent your entire portfolio from losing value at the same time. While one asset falls, another rising or at least staying flat helps preserve your total value. This is measured by the concept of "correlation." Correlation is a statistic that shows the direction and strength of how two assets' prices move together. Its value ranges between -1 and +1. If it is +1, they move in exactly the same direction; if it is -1, in completely opposite directions. For diversification, our aim is to bring together assets with low, even negative, correlation.

2. Why is it important?

What does this knowledge gain you? The most fundamental gain is that it increases your chance of "survival." Markets are unpredictable. A stock that rises today may fall tomorrow on unexpected news. If all your money is in that single stock, this decline would be a disaster for you. Diversification acts as a shield against such sudden and unexpected shocks.

How does this knowledge change your decisions? Whereas before you asked "Which stock earns the most?", now you will ask "Which stocks, when brought together, shake me the least?" Instead of focusing on a single stock, you focus on the performance of your portfolio as a whole. For example, in the data we see TUPRS rose 5.51% daily while TOASO fell 3.53%. If you had only TOASO in your portfolio, you would have closed that day at a loss. But if you also had a stock from a different sector like TUPRS, you could have offset this decline. Diversification may prevent your gains from hitting the ceiling, but it also protects you from hitting rock bottom. In long-term investing, the real goal is not to get rich quickly by taking high risk, but to achieve stable and sustainable growth.

3. How to Read / Use?

To implement portfolio diversification, you must first analyze your current situation. Let's go step by step:

Step 1: List Your Current Portfolio. Write down all the stocks, funds, foreign currencies and other assets you hold in a list.

Step 2: Identify the Sectors. Mark which sector each asset belongs to. (Energy, automotive, telecom, iron-steel, banking, etc.) In our data, TUPRS is in energy, TOASO in automotive, TCELL in telecom, and EREGL in iron-steel.

Step 3: Consider Correlation. Evaluate the relationship between sectors. For example, the automotive and iron-steel sectors are generally related to each other, because steel is the main input of automobile production. Investing in these two sectors may not be very effective in terms of diversification. The "r" values in the data show the correlation of the stocks over a given period. For example, the r=-0.73 value for TUPRS indicates that this stock has a negative-direction relationship with the overall market. This means TUPRS may perform relatively better when the market falls.

Step 4: Diversify Asset Classes. Don't focus only on stocks. Allocate a portion of your portfolio to different asset classes such as gold, foreign currency, and government bonds. This prevents your entire portfolio from losing value even during a general market decline.

Step 5: Establish the Balance. Diversification is not about buying "a little bit of everything." What matters is establishing a balance that suits your risk tolerance. If you like taking high risk, you can hold more stocks in your portfolio; but you should still spread them across different sectors.

4. Real Example

Now let's reinforce what we've learned with examples from our data. Let's say we have a portfolio of 100.000 TL and we're considering investing this money in two stocks. Let's take the EREGL stock in our data. The change in EREGL over the last 30 trading days is given as -13.2%. So this stock has lost value over the past month.

Scenario 1: Putting all the money into EREGL.

  • Portfolio value: 100.000 TL

  • Change over the last 30 days: -13.2%

  • New portfolio value: 100.000 TL - (100.000 TL * 13.2%) = 86.800 TL

  • Result: Since we invested in a single stock, the decline in this stock affected our entire portfolio and we lost 13.200 TL.

Scenario 2: Splitting the money between EREGL and TUPRS.
Let's say we think EREGL will fall and TUPRS is in a relatively better position. However, it's not possible to say anything for certain. So let's split our money in two: 50.000 TL in EREGL, 50.000 TL in TUPRS.

  • EREGL's change over the last 30 days: -13.2%

  • TUPRS's daily change is given as +5.51%. This is only one day's data. However, for this example, let's assume TUPRS also gained 5% over the last 30 days (this is an assumption, not data).

  • New value of the EREGL investment: 50.000 TL - (50.000 TL * 13.2%) = 43.400 TL

  • New value of the TUPRS investment: 50.000 TL + (50.000 TL * 5%) = 52.500 TL

  • Total new portfolio value: 43.400 TL + 52.500 TL = 95.900 TL

  • Result: Our portfolio is still at a loss, but our loss remained at 4.100 TL. This is much less than the 13.200 TL loss in the scenario where we invested in a single stock.

Interpretation: This simple example shows the power of diversification. By investing in two stocks from different sectors (steel and energy), we tried to offset the impact of one stock's decline with the other's rise. The correlation coefficients (r values) in the data tell us how related these stocks are to each other. For example, if TUPRS's r value is -0.73, this means this stock exhibits a movement different from the overall market. This makes it a good candidate for combining with other stocks in our portfolio.

5. Common Mistakes

  1. Over-diversification: " Thinking "the more stocks, the better" is wrong. A small portfolio split across 20 different stocks can be difficult and costly to track. Also, too many stocks can dilute the portfolio's return. Why is it wrong? Because the goal is to bring together a sufficient number of uncorrelated assets to reduce risk; not to buy the entire market.

  2. Investing in the Same Sector: For example, buying both TUPRS and another energy stock is not diversification. Why is it wrong? Because these stocks are likely to be affected similarly by the same news and the same market conditions. When one falls, the other falls too, so you haven't reduced your risk.

  3. Ignoring Correlation: Not buying stocks from different sectors just because they are "different companies". Why is it wrong? Because some sectors may have a hidden relationship with each other. For example, when interest rates rise, both the banking and construction sectors may be negatively affected. Therefore, it is necessary to look not only at sector names but also at correlation coefficients.

6. Mini Test

Question 1: What is the main purpose of portfolio diversification?
Answer: It is the reduction of risk. The goal is to lower the volatility in the total value of the portfolio by ensuring that the depreciation of one asset is balanced by the performance of other assets.

Question 2: What does it mean when two assets have a correlation coefficient of -1?
Answer: It means the prices of these two assets move in completely opposite directions. When one rises, the other falls. This is an ideal situation for diversification.

Question 3: In the given example, the change in EREGL stock over the last 30 trading days is -13.2%. What does this information tell you when making a portfolio decision?
Answer: This information shows that EREGL has exhibited weak performance recently. However, it is not sufficient on its own for an investment decision. When adding this stock to my portfolio, it tells me that I should also evaluate whether this decline will continue and its correlation with other stocks in my portfolio.

7. Summary

  • Portfolio diversification is "not putting all your eggs in the same basket."

  • The goal is to reduce risk by bringing together assets that have low or negative correlation with each other.

  • It is important to invest not only in different companies but also in different sectors and different asset classes.

  • Diversification may prevent you from maximizing gains, but it protects you from large losses.

  • Over-diversification and investing in the same sector are among common mistakes.

8. Deepening

After learning this lesson, your next step should be to understand the concept of correlation more deeply and to learn how to use this information when building your portfolio. For this:

  • Correlation and Covariance: Research these statistical concepts that calculate the degree to which two assets move together.

  • Asset Classes: Examine the characteristics of different asset classes such as stocks, bonds, commodities (gold, oil) and currencies, and their relationships with each other.

  • Practice: Using the "Technical Scan" page on our site, you can examine the correlation coefficients (r values) of stocks from different sectors.
