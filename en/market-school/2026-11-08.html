Today's Lesson: Portfolio diversification

Hello friends, welcome to our weekend stock school! Today we will talk about the best "calming medicine" of the stock market: portfolio diversification. Grab your coffee; first we will set up a picnic basket, then we will do the math with real figures.

1. What is it?

Let's start with this analogy: You go on a picnic and put all the eggs in a single container. If that container falls, breakfast is over. If you distribute the same eggs into three separate containers, even if one falls, the picnic is saved. That is the logic in investing too.

Let's make a clear definition: Portfolio diversification is the art of distributing your savings among different assets with low correlation to each other, at specific weights. So what is correlation? Correlation (denoted by the letter r in statistics) is a coefficient that measures how much two assets move together, and it - between -1 and +1 takes values:

  • + Close to -1: The two move almost hand in hand. When one falls, the other falls too.

  • Close to 0: There is no significant relationship between them. This is the sweet spot of diversification.

  • - Close to -1: They move in opposite directions. When one falls, the other may recover.

The goal is to prevent a single piece of bad news, a single sector crisis, from wiping out your entire savings. Diversification does not guarantee returns; it limits the damage of bad days.

2. Why is it important?

This information concretely gives the investor the following:

  • Protection from single-stock risk: An unexpected development can occur in your favorite stock. In a diversified basket, a single loss is "shared" enough not to topple the overall result.

  • A smoother ride: The basket balances the wild swings of a single stock. A calm chart reduces the likelihood of panic selling. The most expensive mistake in the stock market is selling at the bottom out of fear.

  • A shift in decision quality: Your question changes from "which stock should I buy?" to "who should be in the basket, and how much weight should I give to each?" This is the first step from amateur to serious investor.

  • Real defense against inflation: In an environment where inflation is 29.8% annually, cramming all your money into a single asset means that if that asset lags, your purchasing power also erodes. Different asset classes shine in different periods; the basket brings them into play in turn.

3. How to Read / Use?

Step one: let's build a basket:

Step 1 — Determine your goal and time horizon. " "Down payment for a house in 3 years" and "retirement in 20 years" require very different portfolios. As the time horizon lengthens, the equity share generally increases; as it shortens, cash and interest-bearing instruments gain weight.

Step 2 — Divide into asset classes. Equities, bonds/interest-bearing instruments, gold, foreign exchange, cash... Each class reacts to different winds. Track inflation data from official statistical announcements; use that threshold to compare whether the return in the basket is above inflation.

Step 3 — Calculate the weights. The formula in plain text is as follows:

Weight = Market Value of the Asset / Total Portfolio Value

For example, if an asset's value is 25 units in a 100-unit basket, its weight is 25%. Rule: A single stock should not make up too large a portion of the basket.

Step 4 — Calculate the total change of the basket:

Basket Change = (Change of Asset 1 × Its Weight) + (Change of Asset 2 × Its Weight) +...

You read daily stock changes from tables like the exchange's "most active stocks", and the weights from your own portfolio record.

Step 5 — Look at correlation. You can read stock correlation coefficients (r) from the "r" column in technical screening screens. r is between -1 and +1; the diversification benefit increases when the assets in the basket have a weak or negative relationship with each other.with each other relationship is weak or negative, the benefit increases.

Step 6 — Do periodic rebalancing. Over time, winning positions swell and weights get distorted. Returning to the basket at regular intervals is discipline itself.

4. Real Example

Now let's do the step calculation with today's data.

Step 1 — End-of-day change of an equally weighted three-stock basket. Let's write the formula:

Basket Change = (Change₁ × Weight₁) + (Change₂ × Weight₂) + (Change₃ × Weight₃)

The three most active stocks: TOASO rose 9.12% to 243.00 TRY; ASTOR rose 8.17% to 161.50 TRY; FROTO rose 7.81% to 73.15 TRY. Let's choose equal weights: each 1/3. Let's plug in the numbers:

(9.12% × 1/3) + (8.17% × 1/3) + (7.81% × 1/3) = 3.04% + 2.72% + 2.60% = approximately 8.37%

Comment: Looking at a single day, the basket looks bright. But caution: these three stocks were the day's most active, meaning the most volatile stocks. Moreover, TOASO and FROTO are automotive companies; both are from the same sector. Two stocks from the same sector usually react similarly to similar news. So the "three-stock basket" appearance here does not mean real diversification.
