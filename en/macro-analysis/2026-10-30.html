Macroeconomic Evaluation

30 October 2026, FridayWriting time: 14:53 (GMT+3)Turkey and global macro outlook • AI-supported in-depth analysis 𝕏✈💬🔗

1. Global Macroeconomic Outlook

The two main engines of the global macroeconomic picture — the US and the Eurozone — are displaying a marked asynchronization as they enter the final quarter of 2026. The US economy recorded quarterly growth of 3.4% and annual growth of 2.1% in the second quarter of 2026 (reference period Q2), showing resilience that strengthens the "soft landing" scenario. The engine of this growth is private sector consumption demand, which can be summarized by retail sales rising 0.8% month-on-month and 6.3% year-on-year, and consumer spending remaining strong at 3.2% quarterly. The labor market is still tight: the unemployment rate stood at 4.2% in September, the broad U-6 rate at 7.6%, while JOLTs job openings remained at 7,079 million but with a downward trend. Initial jobless claims are at 194 thousand, continuing claims at 1,701 million, remaining at low levels. This picture shows that the Fed still has room to maneuver under its "stable prices" mandate, but carries the risk that wage pressures (3.0% annual) and core inflation (CPI core 2.6%, PCE core 3.1%) remain above target.

The Fed kept its policy rate unchanged at 4.0% at its meeting on October 29, 2026; however, the September dot plot projections point to 4.1% one year ahead and 3.9% two years ahead, indicating that the "higher for longer" description is still present in market pricing. US bond yields reflect this expectation: 3-month at 3,998%, 5-year at 4,801%, 30-year at 5,329%; the curve demands a higher term premium at the long end. The DXY index stands at 101.98, VIX at 16.47, signaling low volatility and risk appetite. Oil prices (Brent 111.69 USD/barrel, WTI 102.94 USD/barrel) remain elevated, keeping the global inflation risk premium alive.

The Eurozone, on the other hand, is trapped in structural slowdown. Although it recorded quarterly growth of 0.6% and annual growth of 1.3% in the second quarter, August industrial production contracted by 0.5% month-on-month and 1.1% year-on-year; retail sales remained weak at 0.1% monthly and 0.8% annually. Consumer confidence fell to -17.9 points in October, and the economic expectations index to 97.9 points in September; this proves that households and investors' pessimism about the future is increasing. Headline inflation rose to 3.8% annually in September (0.6% monthly), while core inflation at 2.5% still remains above the ECB's 2% target. Producer prices (PPI) rose 8.2% annually and 1.9% monthly in August, indicating cost pressure continues along the pipeline. The ECB kept its policy rate at 2.65%; M3 money supply growth is 3.5%, credit to companies 4.2%, credit to households 3.1%, showing weak expansion in the credit channel. The current account surplus fell to 7.7 billion Euros in August (previous 35.9 billion Euros), while the trade balance posted a deficit of -16.1 billion Euros, reflecting the impact of energy imports and weakening external demand. Unemployment remains steady at 6.4%, but wage growth fell to 3.0% annually in the second quarter; this indicates that second-round effects are under control, though not yet fully eliminated.

In summary; strong growth and sticky inflation in the US keep the Fed cautious about early easing, while in the Eurozone weak growth and declining core inflation could open the path for ECB rate cuts, but structural problems (energy, productivity) will continue to constrain potential growth. This asynchronization will remain the main variable determining global liquidity conditions and capital flows entering emerging markets (EM).

2. Turkey's Macroeconomic Outlook

As of October 30, 2026, the Turkish economy is in the "mid-to-late stage" of the disinflation process; however, this process still has a fragile structure carrying asymmetric risks and requiring continuous calibration from policymakers. The CBRT cut its policy rate by 100 basis points from 37% to 36% at its meeting on October 22, 2026. The logic behind this decision is the disinflation trend confirmed by September CPI data: CPI fell to 29.73% annually (forecast 30.3%, previous 31.51%) and 1.84% monthly (forecast 2.3%, previous 1.84%). PPI was measured at 27.38% annually (previous 27.95%) and 2.07% monthly (previous 2.57%). Monthly inflation coming in below expectations and the marked decline in annual inflation show that the lagged effects of tight monetary policy are bearing fruit. However, when we calculate the real policy rate (36% nominal / 29.73% annual inflation ≈ 4.8% real rate), we see that the policy stance is still in the "restrictive" zone, but the real rate margin is narrowing. If the year-end inflation expectation (24-25% band in the official program) is used for the forward-looking real rate calculation, the real rate could rise to the 10-11% band; this would tighten policy even further.

Growth dynamics continue to slow. In 2026 Q2 (reference period), GDP grew 1.1% quarterly and 2.3% annually (forecast 2.9%, previous 2.6%). Annual growth coming in below expectations shows that domestic demand suppression policies (interest rates, credit costs, real wage erosion) are taking effect, but external demand (exports) and tourism revenues are cushioning this slowdown. Industrial production contracted 1.0% monthly and 1.86% annually in August; the capacity utilization rate stood at 74.5% in October (previous 74.2%), remaining relatively low, signaling investment delays and a closing output gap. The unemployment rate was 8.5% in September; while the labor force participation rate at 52.7% remains above historical averages, the business confidence index fell to 99.8 points in October (previous 102.0) and consumer confidence to 84.8 points (previous 91.9), indicating that households' and firms' future expectations have been seriously damaged. Although retail sales rose 1.4% monthly and 10.1% annually in August, how much of this growth is real volume and how much is price effect must be separated; the consumer loan rate at 64.74% (August data) suggests that durable consumption spending will be suppressed in the coming period.

The current account balance, in the August data (three-month frequency), posted a surplus of 4,053 billion USD (previous ~0), showing a marked improvement in the external balance. Although the September trade deficit remained at 5.16 billion USD (exports 25.97 billion, imports 31.13 billion), the rise in Q2 tourism revenues to 15.9 billion USD (previous 9.9 billion) and the increase in the services balance support the current account. Central bank foreign exchange reserves stood at 62,148 billion USD as of the week of October 22 (previous 64.57 billion); the decline in reserves, despite the current account surplus, may stem from items such as portfolio outflows, banks' swap costs, or gold reserve valuation differences. The real effective exchange rate (REER) rose to 105.45 index points in September (2003=100), showing that the currency has appreciated in real terms, which weakens export competitiveness while lowering imported input costs and helping inflation. USD/TRY is trading at 49.83, EUR/TRY at 55.79; under the managed float regime, the exchange rate follows a path that compensates for the inflation differential, but real appreciation pressure continues.

3. Domestic Demand, Credit and Liquidity

The transmission channel of monetary policy to domestic demand is currently the most effectively functioning mechanism in Turkey. With the CBRT policy rate at 36%, banks' TL deposit cost (weighted cost) stands at 43.04% as of early October data (previous 43.80%), and the consumer loan rate at 64.74% as of August data (previous 63.67%). These interest rate levels show that real rates are positive ex-post and significantly positive ex-ante (expectation-based). Although credit growth appears nominally still high at 36.32% annually as of August data (previous 36.60%), in real terms (based on annual inflation of 29.73%), real credit growth is almost near zero or slightly positive. M3 money supply growth, at 28.65% annually as of early October data (previous 29.78%), remains below inflation (29.73% annual); this proves that the real money supply is contracting and liquidity conditions are tightening.

The transmission mechanism follows this chain: Policy rate hike → Bank funding costs rise (deposit competition, TL-ization incentive) → Loan rates (commercial, consumer, housing) rise → Real credit cost (interest - expected inflation) rises → Firm investment and household durable consumption spending are postponed/canceled → Total domestic demand slows → Capacity utilization falls, unemployment rises → Pricing power weakens → Inflation declines. The data show this chain is working: Capacity utilization is low at 74.5%, industrial production is contracting on an annual basis, consumer/business confidence is breaking down. The 10.1% annual growth in retail sales may still stem from the low base effect of past periods, inventory liquidation, and tourism-driven consumption; forward-looking indicators (confidence indices, credit costs) suggest this growth is not sustainable.

From the banking sector perspective; the high interest rate environment supports the net interest margin (NIM), but the rise in credit risk (NPL) and how much deposit costs can be covered by interest income will be critical. M3 growth remaining below inflation complicates banks' liquidity management; the liquidity supply provided by the CBRT through open market operations (OMO) and reserve requirement decisions will continue to be the main variable determining banks' funding costs.

4. Global Risk Appetite and Interest Rate Expectations

Global risk appetite is characterized by VIX remaining low at 16.47 (previous 16.91), DXY staying relatively stable at 101.98, and US bond yields at the long end (30Y 5.33%, 5Y 4.80%) still demanding a high risk premium. The Fed's September dot plot projections (1 year 4.1%, 2 years 3.9%) cause markets to price in the expectation of a "first rate cut in the first quarter of 2027"; however, the 3-month bond yield at 3,998% (almost equal to the policy rate of 4.00%) shows that markets see a low probability of a cut in the very near term and are basing their assumptions on the "higher for longer" scenario.

This environment creates a two-way effect for emerging markets (EM): 1) High US real yields (5Y real ~1.7%), by increasing the risk premium on EM assets, constrain portfolio flows. 2) Low VIX and relatively stable DXY keep appetite for "carry trade" (interest rate arbitrage) strategies alive; for countries like Turkey with positive real rates and high nominal rates, this can encourage hot money inflows, but these inflows are fragile. As long as the US 10-year real yield (qualitative, as TIPS data is not provided) remains in positive territory, EM local currency borrowing costs stay under pressure.

For Turkey; the CBRT's cut to 36%, combined with the Fed's expected steady rate environment, keeps the TL's carry yield still attractive. However, this attractiveness is conditional on inflation expectations being anchored, the sustainability of the current account surplus, and reserve adequacy. If the Fed realizes a "higher for longer" scenario (e.g., no cuts or only one cut in 2027), DXY would strengthen again, portfolio outflows from EM would accelerate, and the CBRT's rate cut roadmap (forward guidance) would be tested. Key indicators to watch: US October CPI/PCE (early November), the November FOMC meeting, oil price trajectory, and China growth data.

5. Budget and External Balance

The budget balance posted a deficit of 532.2 billion TRY in the September data (previous month: 12.9 billion TRY surplus). This sharp decline may stem from accelerating spending toward year-end (no pre-election effect, but year-end payments), rising interest expenses (domestic borrowing cost), and real erosion of tax revenues. On an annual basis, the budget deficit-to-GDP ratio (qualitative, as GDP data is not in the dataset) carries a risk of target deviation. Financing the budget deficit is met through TL-denominated borrowing from the domestic market (banks, funds); this can narrow banks' capacity to meet public borrowing demand (crowding out) toward private sector loans.

On the external balance side, the picture is more positive: The August period (three-month) current account balance was a surplus of 4,053 billion USD. This is a dramatic recovery from the deep deficits of the 2023-2024 crisis periods (annual ~45-50 billion USD). Main drivers: Tourism revenues hit record levels in Q2 at 15.9 billion USD (previous 9.9 billion); exports held at 25.97 billion USD in September; imports remained at 31.13 billion USD (despite high energy prices). The trade deficit was 5.16 billion USD in September. The strong performance of the services balance (tourism, transportation, software) proves that the current account has begun to structurally generate a surplus.

However, fragilities exist: 1) Energy imports (oil 31.13 USD/barrel) are the biggest risk factor for the current account; if oil rises to 120-130 USD, the current surplus quickly erodes. 2) The short-term external debt stock / reserves ratio (qualitative, no data) may still be at risky levels; reserves are 62.1 billion USD, net reserves (excluding swaps) may be lower. 3) Portfolio flows still constitute a significant part of external financing; if global risk appetite declines (VIX > 25), these flows could reverse. 4) Real exchange rate appreciation (REER 105.45) weakens export competitiveness and encourages imports; for current account sustainability, real appreciation should be controlled through nominal exchange rate management (crawling peg) rather than productivity-driven real appreciation.

6. Energy, Commodities, Foreign Exchange and Bond Markets

Energy markets are the most critical external shock source for global inflation and Turkey's current account balance. Brent oil is at 111.69 USD/barrel (previous 115.32), WTI at 102.94 USD/barrel; OPEC+ supply discipline, China demand uncertainty, and geopolitical risk premiums keep prices in triple digits. Every 10 USD increase in oil prices adds an annual cost of ~1.5-4,053 billion USD to Turkey's current account and contributes ~0.5-0.7 pp to inflation (directly and indirectly). PPI monthly at 2.07% exceeding CPI monthly at 1.84% indicates that energy and raw material costs are not yet fully reflected in consumer prices (incomplete pass-through), carrying the risk of second-round effects.

Gold is at 4.038.30 USD per ounce (previous 4.051.60), gram gold at 6.469.50 TRY (previous 6.486.94). Exchange rates: USD/TRY 49.83 (previous 49.80), EUR/TRY 55.79 (previous 55.44). The TL is almost flat against the USD and has depreciated 0.63% against the Euro. This is consistent with the movement of the EUR/USD parity (around 1,119). The CBRT's exchange rate policy follows a "managed crawl" (crawling peg) that partially offsets the inflation differential (TR ~29.73%, US ~3.7%, EU ~3.8%) and prevents excessive real appreciation (REER). However, the REER level of 105.45 remains above pre-2021 averages (95-100); this shows that support for the external balance through the price competitiveness channel is weak, with quality/diversification (non-price) factors coming to the fore.

In bond markets; US 5Y yield fell to 4.80% (-1.44%), 30Y to 5.33% (-0.60%); the curve is bull steepening (long-end yields falling more). This suggests that Fed rate cut expectations are shifting to the long term, but period-end (rebalancing) and safe haven demand may also be at play. Turkey's local bond yields (not in the dataset) are priced with the CBRT policy rate (36%), inflation expectations, and risk premium components. The high policy rate keeps local bond yields high; this creates profit/loss volatility for banks' Treasury bond portfolios (HTM/AFS).

7. Transmission Mechanisms: From Macro to Market

The transmission of macroeconomic variables to financial markets (BIST, bonds, FX) should be examined by separating it into three main channels:

1. Interest Rate Channel:
Policy Rate (36%) → Bank Deposit/Loan Cost (Deposit 43%, Consumer 65%) → Real Cost of Credit (Nominal Rate - Expected Inflation) → Firm Investment Decision (Discount rate rises in NPV calculation, number of projects falls) + Household Durable Consumption (Housing, auto loan demand contracts) → Total Domestic Demand Slows → Company Revenues Grow Squeezed by Rising Costs (COGS) → Margins (EBITDA margin) Narrow → Cash Flows Fall in DCF Valuation, Discount Rate Rises → Equity Valuations (P/E, EV/EBITDA) Compress.
Matching with data: Credit growth has stopped in real terms, industrial production contracted, confidence indices broke down → Channel is working.

2. Exchange Rate Channel:
Nominal Exchange Rate (USD/TRY 49.83, managed crawl) → Imported Input Costs (Energy, raw materials, intermediate goods) → Producer Prices (PPI Annual 27.38%, Monthly 2.07%) → Consumer Prices (CPI Annual 29.73%, Monthly 1.84%) → Inflation Expectations → CBRT Reaction Function → Policy Rate Path.
Matching with data: Monthly PPI exceeds monthly CPI → Pipeline inflation pressure still exists → The managed crawl of the exchange rate softens this channel, but REER staying at 105 encourages imports.

3. Global Liquidity and Risk Premium Channel (Global Liquidity & Risk Premium):
Fed Rate (4.0%) + US Bond Yields (5Y 4.80%, 30Y 5.33%) + VIX (16.47) → Global Risk Appetite → Foreign Capital Flows (Portfolio: Bond/Equity, Direct Investment) → TL Demand/Supply → Central Reserves (62.1 billion USD) + Bank Open Position → Local Financing Conditions (Swap cost, CDS premium - no data) → BIST Foreign Share (Free Float) → Index Performance.
Matching with data: VIX is low, carry is attractive → Foreign buying can be supported; but if the Fed says "higher for longer," flows reverse, CDS widens, reserves erode, forcing the CBRT's hand.

These three channels feed each other: High rates support the exchange rate (channel 2), if the exchange rate is supported, inflation falls, rate cuts come (channel 1), rate cuts weaken carry, foreigners exit (channel 3). The policy maker's (CBRT/Treasury) balance of this triangle is like "walking a tightrope."

8. Reflections on BIST 30: Sector Channels

BIST 30 (13.961 points, +0.19%) and BIST 100 (11.165 points, +0.13%) traded relatively flat at the October 28 close, while the sectoral dispersion of macro data creates opportunities and risks:

Banking (Weight ~35-40%):
Wins: The spread between the high policy rate (36%) and deposit cost (43%) supports the net interest margin (NIM). TL liraization (deposit growth) drives balance sheet expansion.
Loses: Stalled real credit growth, rising NPL (bad debt) risk (growth 2.3%, unemployment 8.5%), and mark-to-market loss risk in the long-term bond portfolio (HTM/AFS) if yields rise. Foreign investors have high "rate sensitivity" in banks; positioning may stay limited until the Fed cuts.

Industry / Exporters (Automotive, White Goods, Steel, Chemicals):
Loses: REER at 105.45 (real appreciation) erodes price competitiveness. High imported raw material costs (PPI 27.4%) with weak pricing power to pass through (weak domestic demand, external demand contracting with Eurozone industry at -1.1%). Energy costs (Brent 111 USD) squeeze margins.
Wins: If currency depreciation (TL weakening) accelerates (e.g., USD/TRY 55+), operating leverage suddenly expands. The current FX level is not "neutral" but on the "TL strong" side.

Holdings / Diversified (Koc, Sabanci, Isbank etc.):
Portfolio effect dominates. Cash flow from banking arms, margin pressure from industrial arms. Energy, tourism, and retail arms diverge. The tourism arm (Turkey's strongest net FX earner) is strong with Q2 revenue of 15.9 billion USD; but seasonal.

Retail / Consumption-Focused (Supermarkets, Textiles, Auto Sales):
Consumer confidence at 84.8 (broken), consumer loan rates at 65%, real wage growth (no data, qualitative: inflation 0.1%, no official wage increase data but minimum wage hikes etc. imply real wages likely negative/flat) → Durable consumption (white goods, autos) demand is pressured. Supermarkets (food) have low demand elasticity, with risk of a promotion/discount war to protect margins.

Construction / Real Estate (REITs - GYODER):
Housing sales (no data) should have contracted with high loan rates (65%+ estimated mortgage rate) and eroding real income. Renovation (transformation) projects and urban renewal expectations may support valuations, but financing costs are tough.

Summary: In a high-rate / strong-TL / weak-growth regime,Banks > Holdings > Vulnerable Industry > Retail/Construction hierarchy stands out. A regime shift (start of rate cuts, TL weakening) could be a "turnaround" trigger for industry and retail.

9. Scenarios and Monitoring Framework

For the next 6-12 months, we can frame three key scenarios with conditional probabilities (subjective probabilities). These scenarios are not "forecasts" but "scenario planning for risk management."

Base Scenario (Probability ~55%): Gradual Disinflation and Soft Landing

  • Inflation: Year-end 29.73-25, entry into the 15-18% band by 2027. Monthly CPI stays in the 1.5-2.0% range.

  • Rates: CBRT gradually cuts to the 36-32% band in 2027 Q1-Q2 (100-150 bp per meeting). Real rates remain positive.

  • Growth: 2026 GDP 2.5-3.0%, 2027 3.0-3.5% (around potential). Domestic demand subdued, net exports contributing.

  • Current Account: Annual surplus of 1-1.5% of GDP (if oil stays in the 100-115 USD range).

  • FX: Managed drift, REER stabilizes in the 100-105 band.

  • BIST: Banks outperform (NIM peak, falling cost of risk), Industrials underperform (margin pressure), broad market rally from 2027 H2.
    Invalidation: If October/November CPI comes in above 2.5% monthly, or if the Fed does not cut in 2027 / hikes rates.

Optimistic Scenario (Probability ~20%): Rapid Disinflation and Early Pivot

  • Inflation: Supply-side shocks reverse (Oil <90 USD, food prices fall), CPI drops below 20% by year-end.

  • Rates: CBRT starts aggressive cuts below 36% (e.g., 27.5%) in January/February 2027.

  • Capital Flows: Fed cut (March 2027?) + CBRT early cut → Carry trade boom → Foreign inflows of 10-15 bln USD/quarter. Reserves above 70 bln USD.

  • BIST: Broad index (BIST 100) target of 14.000-15.000 points by mid-2027 (25-35% potential from current 11.165). Banks, Industrials, Retail, REITs all outperform.
    Trigger: November CPI below 1.5%, December Fed "pivot" signals, Oil below 90 USD.

Pessimistic Scenario (Probability ~29.73): Sticky Inflation / External Shock / Policy Error

  • Inflation: PPI-CPI gap (PPI monthly 2.07% > CPI 1.84%) pushes CPI to 2.5%+ monthly with second-round effects. Year-end stays above 30%.

  • Rates: Risk emerges that CBRT pauses or even hikes. Real rates turn negative.

  • External Balance: Oil 130+ USD (geopolitical), Eurozone recession → Exports contract, current account turns to deficit. Portfolio outflows (VIX > 30). Reserves fall below 55 bln USD.

  • FX: Sudden TL depreciation (USD/TRY 55-60), REER below 100. Risk of entering an inflation spiral (wage-price spiral).

  • BIST: Banks hit by NPL shock, Industrials margin collapse, BIST 100 tests 10.952-9.500 points. Possibility of liquidity crisis (funding stress).
    Trigger: If November CPI monthly >2.5%, Oil >125 USD, and the Fed's "higher for longer" emphasis strengthens, TCMB communication error (forward guidance deterioration).

3. Quarterly: GDP (2026 Q3 - early December), Current Account Details, Company Financials (Q3 2026 profit/loss, 2027 guidance).

10. Conclusion and Evaluation

As of October 30, 2026, the Turkish economy is in a "transition period" where tight monetary policy is bearing fruit, inflation is on a clear downward trend but still above targets; growth has slowed, the current account balance has structurally improved (via tourism and export discipline), but fragility persists due to real exchange rate appreciation and energy price risks; fiscal policy will remain inconsistent with monetary policy (fiscal dominance risk), and the banking system is trying to balance high interest margins with credit risk.

The TCMB's cut to 36% is consistent with the logic of "calibrated easing" after disinflation is confirmed; however, the market should not price this cut as a "pivot start" (easing cycle start). It should be designed so that the forward-looking real rate remains high. The Fed's "higher for longer" stance strengthens the TCMB's hand (supports carry via interest rate differential) but also tightens global liquidity, keeping EM risk premium elevated.

For BIST; the banking index (XBANK) performance carries a "sell the news" risk in the coming quarters because rate cut expectations are already priced in. For the industrial index (XUSIN) to turn around; either the real exchange rate must decline (TL weakening) or the global industrial cycle (Eurozone/IP) must recover. For retail and construction, real income dynamics and credit costs (rate cuts) will be key.
