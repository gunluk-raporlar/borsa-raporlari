1. Global Macroeconomic Outlook

US and Euro Area data show that the two main engines shaping the global liquidity environment and risk appetite have experienced a clear divergence in the final quarter of 2026. The US Federal Reserve (Fed) kept its policy rate at 4.00, while the September 2026 interest rate projections (dot plot) point to 4.10 for one year ahead and 3.90 for two years ahead. This means the market is pricing in the "higher for longer" scenario, and there are concerns that the terminal rate could be revised upward in upcoming meetings. Inflation data confirms this tension: with August 2026 data, CPI is at 3.4 year-over-year, core CPI at 2.4, PCE at 3.4, and core PCE at 3.0. The distance to the Fed's 2.0 target, especially considering services inflation and wage dynamics (annual wage growth 3.0), suggests that policy easing is being delayed. US growth in the second quarter was 2.2 quarter-over-quarter and 2.2 year-over-year, above potential, while the labor market shows signs of softening: the unemployment rate rose to 4.2 in September (previous 4.1), NFP increased by only 29 thousand (estimate 90 thousand), and JOLTS job openings fell to 7,079 million. Consumer confidence dropped to 81.9 in September, far below the estimate (89.2); this is an indication of how high interest rates and cumulative inflation are eroding household sentiment. Retail sales grew 1.2 month-over-month and 6.0 year-over-year in August, maintaining resilience, but how much of this growth is real versus price-driven is debatable. Industrial production expanded slightly at 0.2 month-over-month and 1.4 year-over-year; capacity utilization is at 76.3.

On the Euro Area side, the ECB policy rate is fixed at 2.65. With September data, headline inflation is 3.8 year-over-year and core inflation is 2.5; both are above target. Producer prices (PPI) showed double-digit growth of 8.2 year-over-year in August; this indicates that energy and intermediate input costs still carry the risk of transmission to processing chains. Growth in the second quarter was 0.6 quarter-over-quarter and 1.2 year-over-year, corresponding to half of the US. Industrial production was -0.1 month-over-month in July and 0.1 year-over-year in June, on the brink of stagnation. Unemployment is steady at 6.4; wage growth of 3.0 year-over-year in the second quarter is competing with inflation. Consumer confidence is -16.5, and the economic expectations index is 97.9, with both household and firm channels in the pessimism zone. The current account balance posted a surplus of 36.5 billion euros in July, and the trade balance a surplus of 14.2 billion euros; a structure where external balance does not support growth, and net exports could even have a negative contribution. M3 money supply growth is 3.5 year-over-year, corporate loans 4.2, and household loans 3.1, with the credit channel weak but not broken.

In terms of global liquidity, DXY is at 102.20, VIX at 15.27, with the "fear index" below historical averages, while the US 30-year Treasury yield is at 5.56, 5-year at 5.01, and 3-month at 4.06. This part of the curve (the belly) shows that early Fed rate cut expectations are not being priced in, and the term premium and inflation risk premium remain high. Brent at 106.46 and WTI at 94.89 dollars/barrel, combined with OPEC+ discipline and China demand uncertainty, represent an upside risk factor for global inflation. Gold at 4182 dollars per ounce and gram gold at 6634 TL continue to function as a safe haven in environments where real rates remain negative/positive real returns are weak. This picture draws a fragile balance for emerging market assets, where the "carry" appeal is supported by high interest rate differentials, but "duration" risk (long-term bond yields) could explode at any moment.

2. Turkey's Macroeconomic Outlook

The CBRT policy rate was kept at 37.0 at the September 2026 meeting. With September TUIK data, CPI is 1.84 month-over-month and 29.73 year-over-year; PPI is 2.07 month-over-month and 27.38 year-over-year. Annual inflation fell from 31.51 in August to 29.73; monthly inflation remained at the same level as the previous month at 1.84. This indicates that the disinflation process has entered the "disinflation slowdown" phase, meaning that even though the cumulative effect declines within the year, monthly momentum still cannot fall below 2, and structural stickiness persists in services and processed food subgroups. The real policy rate (ex-post) is approximately 37.0 - 29.73 = 7.27 percentage points positive; the ex-ante (expectation-based) real rate, when calculated based on next 12-month inflation expectations, could be lower. The CBRT keeping the rate at this level within the "tight monetary policy" framework is necessary but not sufficient to anchor inflation expectations and protect the TL; in the background, financial conditions need to tighten (credit growth, real credit growth) and a permanent decline in inflation needs to be achieved through the demand channel.

Growth in the second quarter was 1.1 quarter-over-quarter and 2.3 year-over-year; there is a recovery after the first quarter's 0.3 quarter-over-quarter growth, but on an annual basis we are below potential growth (approximately 3.5-4.0). Industrial production continued to contract in August at -1.0 month-over-month and -1.86 year-over-year; despite capacity utilization rising to 74.2 in September (previous 73.5), the weakness in industrial activity is a combination of domestic demand pressure and weak external demand (EU growth 1.2). Unemployment is 7.7 in August; the economic participation rate is low at 52.5. Consumer confidence is 91.9, business confidence 102.0, and the economic expectations index 101.3; confidence indices are hovering around the 101.3 threshold, with households pessimistic and firms slightly optimistic. Retail sales in August were 1.4 month-over-month and 10.1 year-over-year; nominal growth is strong, but when we calculate real retail sales (deflating with CPI), they should be almost flat or slightly increasing within the year. This is a picture where high interest rates, reflected in consumer loan costs (64.74), suppress durable consumption, but services spending supported by real wage improvements and tourism revenues (Q2 15.9 billion dollars) balances it out.

3. Domestic Demand, Credit and Liquidity

With August data, the banking sector deposit rate is 43.3 and the consumer loan rate is 64.74. There is a margin of ~630 basis points from the policy rate (37.0) to the deposit rate, and ~2770 basis points to consumer loans; this reflects banks' risk premium, operating expenses, and BRSA macroprudential measures (e.g., increased risk weights on consumer loans). While the loan stock grew 36.32 year-over-year (previous 36.60), M3 money supply grew 26.11 year-over-year (October 1 data, previous 29.78). If nominal credit growth is above inflation (29.73), real credit growth is still positive but narrowing; M3 growth remains below inflation, indicating that real money supply is tightening and liquidity is contracting in real terms. The transmission channel works as follows: policy rate increase -> bank funding costs rise -> deposit rates rise (43.3) -> loan rates (commercial/public/housing) rise -> new loan demand decreases, interest payments on existing loans squeeze household/firm cash flows -> consumption and investment expenditures are postponed/cancelled -> aggregate demand softens -> firm sales and margins are pressured -> employment and wage pressure decreases -> inflation falls. The data show that the "deposit/loan cost -> domestic demand" leg of this chain is working (retail sales weak in real terms, industrial production contracting), but the "domestic demand -> inflation" leg is working slowly on the services side (CPI monthly 1.84 still high). Bank credit growth remaining at 36 levels suggests that despite positive real rates, nominal rate expectations and inflation hedging demand (no flight from TL, seeking high returns in TL) keep credit demand alive.

4. Global Risk Appetite and Interest Rate Expectations

VIX 15.27, DXY 102.20, US 10-year Treasury yield (5-year 5.01 proxy) above 5. Fed rate projections (1 year 4.10, 2 years 3.90) show that the market's expectation for the "start of cuts" is postponed to the first quarter of 2027 or later. High-yield credit spreads (not in the data block, qualitative) and investment-grade spreads may have narrowed, but this level of VIX suggests that the "soft landing" scenario is being priced in and crisis risk is seen as low. BIST 100 rose 0.82, BIST 30 rose 1.27; foreign investor positioning (no data) may have turned from net selling to buying in recent months; high real rates (37 vs 29.73) increase the appeal of carry trade. However, US long-term yields staying at 5.50+ levels keeps new market borrowing costs (Eurobond issues) high and creates costs for CBRT reserve management. Risk appetite reflected in BIST: the banking index tends to outperform with margin expansion, while the industrial/retail index tends to underperform due to cost pressure and weak demand. With VIX below 15.27, EM flows can remain positive; a sudden spike above 15.27 (geopolitical, US data shock) triggers "sudden stop" risk.

5. Budget and External Balance

August budget balance was a surplus of 12.9 billion TL (previous -378.1 billion TL). This dramatic turnaround is likely a "window dressing" where expenditures are postponed to meet year-end targets, revenues (VAT, SCT, stamp duty) are inflated by nominal inflation, and fund operations (university/hospital funds, etc.) are brought forward. On an annual basis, the budget deficit should remain around 5-6% of GDP (no data, qualitative). The current account balance in July was a surplus of 36 million dollars (estimate 650 million dollars, previous -4.33 billion dollars); almost balanced. The trade balance in September was -5.23 billion dollars (exports 25.98, imports 31.21). Energy imports (no data, qualitative) account for ~25-30% of imports; Brent at 106 could cause the current account to swing back into deficit towards year-end. Tourism revenues were very strong at 15.9 billion dollars in Q2 (previous 9.9); the seasonal effect will continue in Q3 but decline in Q4. Foreign exchange reserves are 60.33 billion dollars (October 2, previous 61.56); net reserves (excluding swaps) are much lower. Short-term external debt stock (no data) could be 1.5-2 times reserves; this shows that external financing fragility remains high and the current account is vulnerable to "energy price + interest payments" shocks. The real effective exchange rate index at 105.45 (September) on a 2003=0,036 basis is still overvalued; this makes structural improvement in the current account difficult, harms exporter profitability, and delays the "J-curve" effect.

6. Energy, Commodities, Foreign Exchange and Bond Markets

Brent 106.46, WTI 94.89; both oils are high. Turkey is a net energy importer; every 10-dollar increase in oil prices expands the current account deficit by ~6-7 billion dollars per year, contributes ~1.5-2 points to PPI and ~0.5-1 point to CPI with a lag. Gold at 4182 dollars per ounce, gram gold at 6634 TL; gold in TL, when calculated at the USD/TRY 49.34 rate (4182 * 49.34 / 31,1035 ≈ 6634), moves exactly with global prices and exchange rates; local demand premium (piggy bank gold) is low. USD/TRY 49.34, EUR/TRY 55.31; exchange rates have shown "managed" stability in a narrow range (49.30-49.40) in recent weeks. The CBRT's net reserve sales (61.56 -> 60.33) are the cost of this stability. US Treasury yields (30Y 5.56, 5Y 5.01) serve as a reference for Turkish Eurobond yields (no data); the CDS premium (no data) should remain in the 300-350 bp band. High oil + high US yields + strong DXY = "triple squeeze" for EM. For Turkey, this creates a dilemma to maintain its current account: either allow real appreciation of the TL (reduces inflation but hits exports) or nominal crawling peg protection (burns reserves, increases inflation risk).

7. Transmission Mechanisms: From Macro to Market

The three main chains are structured as follows through the data:

  1. Interest Rate -> Credit Cost -> Domestic Demand -> Company Sales/Margins -> Valuation: Policy rate 37.0 -> Deposit 43.3 / Consumer loan 64.74 -> Real credit growth slows (M3 contracts in real terms) -> Retail sales weaken in real terms (10.1 nominal annual, ~ -15 real when deflated with CPI 29.73) -> Industrial production -1.86, Capacity utilization 74.2 -> Firm sales volume falls, unit cost (PPI 27.38) rises slower than selling price (CPI 29.73) but margins narrow -> BIST industrial/retail indices are highly interest-sensitive, banking index is profitable with NIM (net interest margin) expansion (loan 64.74 - deposit 43.3 ≈ 2140 bp gross margin) but credit costs (NPL) risk increasing.

  2. Exchange Rate Channel -> Imported Input Cost -> Inflation: USD/TRY 49.34, REER 105.45 -> Import input prices (PPI 27.38) fluctuate depending on exchange rate movements. If the exchange rate is stable (currently it is), PPI pressure decreases, pass-through to CPI occurs with a lag of 6-9 months at a coefficient of ~0.3-0.4. If oil rises above 110+ and/or TRY goes above 50+, PPI could rise above 30+, disinflation could stall.

  3. Global Interest -> Foreign Capital Flows -> Risk Premium: Fed 4.00, ECB 2.65, TCMB 37.0 -> Interest rate differential ~3300 bp (vs Fed), ~3435 bp (vs ECB). This is attractive for short-term portfolio flows (carry). However, US 5Y 5.01, 30Y 5.56 -> long-term real yields are high -> EM risk premium (CDS) does not narrow, it may even widen. Foreign investors are playing "carry + currency option" in BIST; if currency risk perception changes (e.g., reserves below 55, current account deficit above $1 billion), sudden stop -> TRY depreciation -> inflation -> TCMB rate hike cycle kicks in.

8. Reflections on BIST 30: Sector Channels

  • Banking: The most direct interest rate channel. Deposits 43.3, loans 64.74 -> NIM at historical peaks. High-interest deposit-based funding (low demand deposit share) sustains the margin. Risk: NPL ratio (no data) was in the 1.5-2.0 band in 2024-25, with real growth 2.3 and real interest 7+, it could rise to 3-4 in 2026-27. Valuation: P/B 0.8-1.0, P/E 4-5, cheap but there is a "value trap" risk.

  • Industry/Production (Automotive, White Goods, Steel, Chemicals): Capacity utilization 74.2, industrial production -1.86, PPI 27.38 > CPI 29.73 (margin pressure). Export-oriented companies (Ford Otosan, Tofaş, Ereğli, Petkim) are losing competitiveness with REER 105.45. High working capital cost (loans 50-60+) increases inventory costs. Only companies with hard currency (USD/EUR) revenues, low debt, and pricing power are resilient.

  • Holdings: Koç, Sabancı, İş Bankası etc. Portfolio effect: bank profits increase, industrial profits decrease, net effect neutral/positive. Holding discount (NAV discount) may narrow in an interest rate environment (cash flow visibility increases).

  • Retail/Consumption (BIM, Mavi, Migros): Retail sales are weak in real terms, consumer confidence 91.9. High credit cost (64.74) is paralyzing durable consumption (white goods, automotive, furniture). Food retail (BIM) outperforms with inflation protection (pricing power); fashion/footwear (Mavi) can sustain margins while under volume pressure.

  • Construction/Real Estate (Emlak Konut, İş GYO): Mortgage interest rate (no data, estimated ~50-55) is suppressing demand; sales have shifted to cash/down payment. Real estate investment trusts (REITs) have rental income indexed to inflation (CPI/PPI), debts are mixed TRY/USD; real return is positive but valuation (NAV) is discounted with interest rate increases.

10. Conclusion and Evaluation

The data shows that, within the framework of Türkiye's "tight monetary policy", the economy is making slow but fragile progress on the disinflation path with positive real interest rates; however, momentum is still maintained in the structural components of inflation (services, rent, food), industrial production is contracting, and the external balance remains sensitive to energy prices and real exchange rate appreciation. In the global environment, the Fed's "higher for longer" stance and high long-term yields keep the liquidity cost high for emerging market economies, while the appeal of carry trade supports short-term flows. For BIST 30, this means that the banking-heavy index is supported by the interest margin story, while industrial/retail channels come under pressure from demand and cost squeezes. The critical distinction for the investor: instead of chasing nominal returns, a real return-focused (net of inflation and currency risk) approach based on sector selection and balance sheet quality (debt/equity, cash flow, pricing power) is required.
