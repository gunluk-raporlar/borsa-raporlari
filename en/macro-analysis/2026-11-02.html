1. Global Macroeconomic Outlook

The global economy, entering the final quarter of 2026, is going through a period in which the final stages of the fight against inflation and the loss of growth momentum occur simultaneously, and the policy paths of the two major central banks (Fed and ECB) diverge. This divergence is one of the fundamental factors directly shaping global liquidity conditions and risk appetite.

On the US side, the outlook shows the "soft landing" scenario is still on the table but increasingly difficult. The US economy grew 2.1% annually in the third quarter and 3.4% quarterly with a strong performance. This strong growth figure points to resilient domestic demand, but also keeps inflationary pressures alive. So headline annual inflation in September rose to 3.7%, above the previous period's 3.4%, while core inflation remained above target at 2.6%. The core PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred gauge, rising to 3.1% annually confirms the breadth and persistence of price pressures. The outlook on producer prices is even more concerning; in September, monthly PPI rose 1.3%, annual PPI 6.3%, well above expectations. These data show cost pressures have not yet broken and may spill over to consumer prices in the coming months.

The labor market, in turn, gives a two-directional signal. On the one hand the unemployment rate runs at historically low levels with 4.2%, on the other hand it is an important sign that the labor market's cooling has begun that the nonfarm employment increase stayed well below the expectations (90 thousand) in September with only 29 thousand people. That the monthly wage increase remains limited to 0.1% suggests the wage-inflation spiral is under control for now, yet the fall in the JOLTs job openings number (7.079 million) and the level in the ongoing unemployment applications show the demand's weakening has begun seeping into the labor market. This picture prepares a ground supporting the Fed's holding its policy rate fixed at 4.0%. However, it is a strong signal that the rapid rate cuts the market expects will not materialize that the Fed foresees in its September projections the rate being 4.1% a year later and 3.9% two years later. This has also reflected on the long-term bond yields, the US 30-year bond yield has held at high levels with 5.31%.

The euro area paints a different picture from the US. Although the region's economy recorded annual growth of 1.3% in the third quarter, the quality of this growth is questionable. The contraction in industrial production (monthly -0.5%, annual -1.1%) and the trade balance posting a deficit of -16.1 billion euros in August point to fragility in the manufacturing sector. By contrast, the services sector and domestic demand are relatively resilient; retail sales rose 0.1% month-on-month, and the unemployment rate held at a low 6.5%. However, the inflation front remains a serious problem for the European Central Bank (ECB). In September, annual inflation rose to 3.8%, while core inflation came in at 2.5%. Even more critical is the very high annual PPI of 8.2%. This data show that increases in energy and commodity prices have significantly inflated producer costs and that this cost pressure will pass through to consumer prices in the period ahead. The ECB's decision to keep the policy rate unchanged at 2.65% expresses a cautious stance against this inflationary outlook. However, the ECB, squeezed between signs of economic slowdown (decline in consumer confidence: -17.9 points, decline in the economic expectations index: 96.9 points) and inflation, will need to see a lasting decline in inflation before starting rate cuts.

In terms of global liquidity conditions, the postponement of rate cuts by both the Fed and the ECB indicates that external financing conditions for developing countries will remain tight. The US 5-year bond yield at a high level of 4.72% puts pressure on the global risk premium. This environment is the main factor limiting risk appetite for emerging market assets.

2. Turkey's Macroeconomic Outlook

The economy of Türkiye is in a transition period in the last quarter of 2026, in which, on the one hand, the disinflation process continues and, on the other, the slowdown in growth becomes pronounced. The most critical feature of this transition period is that, while the tight stance in monetary policy cools domestic demand, supply-side cost pressures (especially energy- and exchange-rate-related) continue to hold inflation up.

According to TÜİK data, annual CPI inflation in September fell to 29.73% from the previous month's 31.51% level, while monthly inflation came in at 1.84%. This data shows the disinflation process continues but its pace is slowing. The annual PPI inflation falling to 27.38% indicates the pressure on producer prices eased somewhat. However, the monthly PPI at 2.07% coming in above the CPI shows the cost-driven inflationary pressure continues and this pressure may reflect onto consumer prices in the coming months. This picture reveals that the "easy gains" stage of the disinflation process has been completed and the declines ahead will be slower and harder.

In this process, the TCMB cut the policy rate by 100 basis points in October, bringing it to 36.0%. This cut can be evaluated as a gradual normalization step aimed at supporting the disinflation process in an environment where the real interest rate is still quite high. However, it can be said that, in an environment where inflation is 29.73%, the rate cut ensures that the real interest rate remains positive. This reflects the TCMB's strategy of "gradual normalization while maintaining the tight stance".

On the growth side, the slowdown is clear. In the second quarter, annual growth came in below expectations at 2.3% (2.9%), with quarterly growth of 1.1%. Behind this slowdown lie high-interest-rate suppression of domestic demand and a contraction in industrial production. In August, industrial production contracted %-1.0 monthly and %-1.86 annually. Capacity utilization staying low at 74.5% points to continued weakness in the industrial sector. The unemployment rate rising to 8.5% shows the growth slowdown is spilling into the labor market. The sharp fall in consumer confidence (84.8 points) and the decline in the economic sentiment index (97.3 points) give leading signals that domestic demand may weaken further in the coming period.

This table shows the Turkish economy faces a "stagflation" risk: while growth slows, inflation remains at high levels. This requires a difficult balance from a monetary policy perspective: continuing rate cuts may support growth but increase inflationary pressures; holding rates steady supports the fight against inflation but deepens the growth slowdown.

3. Domestic Demand, Credit and Liquidity

The transmission channel extending from monetary policy to domestic demand is currently the most critical mechanism in the Türkiye economy. While the TCMB policy rate stands at 36.0%, the reflection of this rate into credit and deposit costs is the fundamental factor determining the direction of domestic demand.

According to the data, the deposit rate stands at 43.04% as of October. This rate offers a return well above the policy rate, encouraging savers to turn to TL assets and easing the pressure on the exchange rate. However, at the same time it raises the banks' funding cost, causing loan rates to remain high. Indeed, as of August the consumer loan rate is at a very high level of 64.74%. This rate seriously restricts household credit usage and suppresses domestic demand, especially for durable consumer goods.

Credit growth data show the effect of this pressure. Annual credit growth appears high at 35.29% in September, but much of this growth is inflation-driven. In real terms, credit growth is contracting by staying below inflation. The decline in the annual increase in M3 money supply to 28.65% confirms that money supply growth is slowing and liquidity conditions are tightening.

This table shows domestic demand is gradually cooling. That retail sales rose 1.4% monthly may suggest consumption is still lively, but much of this increase stems from price rises. The annual retail sales increase of 10.1% indicates consumption is almost flat in real terms. The sharp fall in consumer confidence shows households' expectations for the future have soured and consumption spending may slow further in the coming period.

The suppressing effect of the high interest environment on domestic demand also reflects onto the corporate sector. The job confidence index falling to 99.8 points and the capacity utilization remaining at low levels show firms act prudently in their investment and production decisions. This signals economic growth may slow further in the coming period.

4. Global Risk Appetite and Interest Rate Expectations

Global risk appetite follows a volatile course in the final quarter of 2026, shadowed by the tug-of-war between inflation and rate expectations. The VIX rising to 19.42 signals increased uncertainty and risk aversion in markets. This increase is closely tied to US inflation data coming in above expectations and the growing likelihood that the Fed postpones rate cuts.

The dollar index (DXY) moves sideways at 101.88. This shows the dollar remains strong globally, but upside is limited. US bond yields hold at high levels due to tightened rate expectations. The 5-year bond yield is at 4.72%, the 30-year at 5.31%. These high yields make US assets attractive to global investors and limit capital flows to emerging countries.

The Fed's rate projections point to a higher rate path than the market expects. Fed members' rate expectation is 4.1% for one year ahead and 3.9% for two years ahead. These projections show that the Fed will not rush to begin rate cuts and will want to see a sustained decline in inflation. This means that external financing conditions for emerging countries will remain tight.

This limited outlook in global risk appetite creates direct pressure on BIST. High US bond yields reduce foreign investors' appetite for emerging markets like Türkiye and limit portfolio flows. This is one of the factors behind the recent decline in the BIST 100 index (%-3.64).

5. Budget and External Balance

In evaluating Türkiye's vulnerabilities, fiscal discipline and external balance items carry critical importance. In September, the budget balance posted a marked deficit of -532.2 billion TL. This deficit means a sharp deterioration compared to the previous month's surplus of 12.9 billion TL. This deterioration may stem from rising interest expenses and the increase in public spending. In a high interest environment, the rise in public borrowing costs increases the pressure on the budget. This narrows the maneuvering room of fiscal policy and blocks the fiscal tightening that would support the disinflation process.

On the external balance side, there is a more positive picture. In August, the current account closed with a surplus of 4.053 billion dollars. This surplus indicates Türkiye's external financing need has decreased. Behind this improvement in the current account lie the exports that reached 25.97 billion dollars in September and tourism revenues of 25.5 billion dollars. However, imports running above exports at 31.13 billion dollars cause a trade deficit of -5.16 billion dollars. This deficit shows the current account surplus rests largely on tourism revenues.

FX reserves fell to 59.045 billion dollars as of October. This decline shows reserve accumulation has slowed and FX liquidity is limited. This level of reserves is well below a safety margin to act as a buffer against external shocks. This situation indicates Türkiye's external financing fragility persists. Despite the surplus in the current account, the low level of reserves shows that pressure on the exchange rate may persist.

6. Energy, Commodities, Foreign Exchange and Bond Markets

Energy prices remain one of the most critical determinants of global inflationary pressures and Türkiye's current account balance. Brent crude is at 113.43 dollars/barrel, while WTI is at 104.12 dollars/barrel. These high prices show that geopolitical risks and supply constraints persist. High energy prices create inflationary pressure for energy-importing countries such as Türkiye both through the current account deficit and through production costs. This high level of energy prices was also a key factor in pushing the euro area PPI up to 8.2%.

Gold prices recorded a marked decline recently at 3,898.90 dollars/ounce. This decline stems from high US bond yields raising the opportunity cost of gold. Gram gold fell to 6,259.65 TL. Both the decline in ounce prices and the flat exchange rate were effective in this fall.

On the foreign exchange side, USD/TRY is at 49.94 TL, EUR/TRY at 55.76 TL. This relatively sideways course of the exchange rate stems from high deposit rates making TL assets attractive and the TCMB's reserve management policies. The real effective exchange rate index being at 105.45 shows the TL is appreciated in real terms. This situation cheapens imports but may hurt export competitiveness.

Looking at the exchange rate-inflation interaction, the sideways course of the currency contributes to easing inflationary pressures. However, high energy prices and imported input costs partially offset this positive effect. Whether the exchange rate can maintain its sideways course will be critical to the pace of disinflation in the period ahead.

7. Transmission Mechanisms: From Macro to Market

The effect of macroeconomic indicators on financial markets occurs through a series of transmission channels. Understanding how these channels work is essential for interpreting market movements.

Policy Rate -> Deposit/Credit Cost -> Domestic Demand -> Company Sales -> Margins -> Valuation Chain: The TCMB policy rate stands at the 36.0% level. This rate determines the banks' funding cost and directly affects the deposit rates (43.04%) and loan rates (64.74%). High loan rates raise the borrowing cost of households and firms, suppressing domestic demand. The slowdown in domestic demand negatively affects companies' sales revenues, causing profit margins to narrow. Narrowing margins reduce the present value of companies' future cash flows, negatively affecting equity valuations. This chain is the fundamental explanation of the recent decline (%-3.88) in the BIST 30 index.

Exchange Rate Channel -> Imported Input Cost -> Inflation: Exchange-rate movements directly affect the costs of firms using imported inputs. USD/TRY moving sideways at 49.94 TL ensures inflationary pressure from this channel remains limited. However, a possible upward move in the exchange rate would raise imported input costs and exert upward pressure on PPI and TÜFE. Therefore, exchange-rate stability is of critical importance for the continuation of the disinflation process.

Global Interest Rates -> Foreign Capital Flow -> Risk Premium: US bond yields staying at high levels limits capital flows to emerging countries. High US rates make US assets more attractive to foreign investors, reducing portfolio flows to countries like Türkiye. Diminishing capital flows keep Türkiye's risk premium high and financial conditions tight. This situation puts pressure on BIST and may trigger an upward move in the exchange rate.

8. Reflections on BIST 30: Sector Channels

Since the BIST 30 index houses companies from different sectors, it is asymmetrically affected by macroeconomic developments. In an environment of high rates and slowing growth, a marked divergence between sectors is expected.

Banking Sector: The high interest environment creates a two-sided effect for the banks. On the one hand, the deposit rates being high increases the banks' funding cost. On the other hand, the loan rates being high supports the banks' credit profitability. However, the slowdown in credit growth and the rising non-performing receivables risk create a negative pressure on the banks' profitability. The consumer loan rate being at a very high level like 64.74% restricts the banks' credit volume growth by curbing credit demand. Therefore, the banking sector's performance depends on the balance between the net interest margin and credit risk.

Industrial sector: The contraction in industrial production (annually -1.86%) and the low level of capacity utilization (74.5%) point to a difficult environment for industrial companies. High interest rates postpone investment decisions, while the slowdown in domestic demand negatively affects sales. Moreover, high energy prices and imported input costs raise production costs and narrow profit margins. This picture shows that the stock performance of industrial companies depends on recovery signals in domestic demand and the easing of cost pressures.

Holding Companies: Holding companies offer a diversified risk profile depending on the performance of their subsidiaries across different sectors. While the high interest rate environment increases holdings' financing costs, slowing growth negatively affects their subsidiaries' sales revenues. However, the presence of energy- or export-oriented companies in holdings' portfolios can partially offset these negative effects.

Retail sector: The annual increase in retail sales at 10.1% shows that consumption is almost flat in real terms. High credit interest rates suppress demand for durable consumer goods, which negatively affects the sales of retail companies. The decline in consumer confidence points to an even more difficult environment for the retail sector in the coming period.

9. Scenarios and Monitoring Framework

In light of current data, it is possible to construct three different scenarios for the coming period. These scenarios will take shape depending on specific data developments.

Base Scenario: In this scenario, it is assumed that the disinflation process will continue slowly but steadily, the TCMB will continue its gradual rate cuts, and the exchange rate will maintain its sideways course. In that case, the slowdown in domestic demand will continue, growth will stay around 2.3%, and inflation may fall to 29.73%-27 by year-end. The BIST 30 index may record a limited recovery from current levels in this scenario. A development that would invalidate this scenario is monthly inflation coming in above expectations or a marked upward movement in the exchange rate.

Optimistic Scenario: In this scenario, it is assumed that global energy prices fall markedly and US inflation declines rapidly, with the Fed starting rate cuts. In that case, global risk appetite will rise and capital flows to emerging markets will accelerate. In Türkiye, disinflation is expected to accelerate, the TCMB to speed up rate cuts, and domestic demand to revive. In this scenario, the BIST 30 index may rise significantly. A development that would invalidate this scenario is inflation falling slower than expected or global risk appetite not increasing.

Pessimistic Scenario: In this scenario, it is assumed that global energy prices rise even further and geopolitical risks increase. In that case, global inflationary pressures will increase, central banks will have to postpone rate cuts. In Türkiye, the rising energy import bill will spoil the current balance and put pressure on the exchange rate. This situation may, by disrupting inflation expectations, hinder the disinflation process. In this scenario, the BIST 30 index may fall significantly. A development that would invalidate this scenario is a marked decline in energy prices or the exchange rate maintaining its sideways course.

The fundamental indicators to be monitored in the coming period are: the monthly CPI and PPI data, the TCMB's rate decisions and verbal guidances, the currency movements, the reserve changes, the credit growth and the deposit rates, the US inflation data and the Fed's rate expectations, the Brent oil prices and the geopolitical developments.

10. Conclusion and Evaluation

The economy of Türkiye is going through a transition period in the last quarter of 2026 in which the disinflation process continues but growth slows markedly. The TCMB's tight monetary policy stance lowers inflation by cooling domestic demand, but this also suppresses economic activity. The high interest environment limits consumption and investment by raising credit costs, which creates a negative pressure on company profitability and equity valuations.

On the global side, continued inflationary pressures in the US and the Eurozone cause central banks to postpone rate cuts, which in turn keeps external financing conditions tight for developing countries. This increases Türkiye's external vulnerability and creates pressure on financial markets.

In the coming period, the speed of the disinflation process, the course of the currency and the global risk appetite will be the fundamental factors determining the direction of the Türkiye economy and the BIST 30 index. In this environment of uncertainty, it is of great importance that the investors are cautious and adopt a data-oriented approach.
