2. Turkey's Macroeconomic Outlook

The Turkish economy grew 2.3% annually and 1.1% quarterly in the second quarter of 2026; annual growth came in below the estimate (2.9%) and also declined from the previous quarter's 2.6%. This shows that the cooling effect of tight monetary policy on domestic demand is materializing. Industrial production in August data is -1.0% monthly and -1.86% annually; the capacity utilization rate in October is 74.5% (previous 74.2) — there is a slight recovery, but industry is still in contraction territory. Retail sales appear resilient with a monthly increase of 1.4% and annual increase of 10.1% in August; however, this can also be fueled by improvements in real wages and early consumption (purchases before price increases) dynamics. Consumer confidence fell to 84.8 points in October (previous 91.9), and business confidence declined to 99.8 points (previous 102.0); household and firm expectations have cooled significantly.

Significant progress has been made in the inflation fight: CPI is 29.73% annually (September, below the estimate of 30.3%), down from the previous 31.51%; monthly 1.84% (below the estimate of 2.3%). PPI is 27.38% annually (previous 27.95), monthly 2.07% (previous 2.57). Core inflation data is not separately included in the dataset, but the narrowing gap between headline and PPI suggests that cost pressure is also slowing. The real effective exchange rate index in September is 105.45 (previous 105.0) — the TL's real depreciation continues but at a slowing pace. The CBRT cut its policy rate by 100 basis points to 36.0% (previous 37.0) at the October meeting; this shows that the disinflation process is moving toward "medium-term" targets (annual 20-25% band), but the "last mile" (single-digit inflation) is still far away. Deposit rate is 43.04%, consumer loan rate is 64.74% — real rates (based on annual inflation of 29.73%) are still strongly positive; this indicates that savings incentives and credit demand suppression channels are working.

M3 money supply is 28.65% annually (previous 29.78) — money supply growth is below inflation, and real money supply is contracting. Credit growth is 36.32% annually (previous 36.60) — nominally high but tending to contract in real terms. The unemployment rate in August data is 8.5%; the labor force participation rate is 52.5%. The labor market still shows slack; this helps break the wage-inflation spiral but also limits potential growth.

The disinflation process is transitioning from the "first phase" (decline in shock inflation) to the "second phase" (slowing of persistent components and services inflation). In the process led by the CBRT: (1) the policy rate has been kept positive in real terms, (2) exchange rate management has been kept aligned with market conditions rather than provocative, (3) macroprudential tools (credit limits, guarantee ratios) have selectively directed credit growth. Risks: (a) stickiness in services inflation, (b) pass-through of real exchange rate depreciation to imported input costs, (c) the budget deficit conflicting with monetary policy, (d) portfolio outflows in the event of a sudden closure in global risk appetite.

3. Domestic Demand, Credit and Liquidity

Indicators available in the dataset: policy rate 36.0%; deposit rate 43.04%; consumer loan rate 64.74%; M3 money supply 28.65% annually; credit growth 36.32% annually. These figures concretize how the transmission mechanism is working.

The pass-through from the policy rate to deposit rates is relatively fast and almost one-to-one: banks shape their TL funding costs around the policy rate and TLREF (TL overnight reference rate); deposit rates stay above the policy rate (currently 36 percentage points above). This keeps households' incentive to hold savings in TL strong and breaks the dollarization trend. The pass-through from deposit rates to loan rates, however, creates a wider spread due to risk premiums, operating expenses, and profit margins: consumer loan rates are ~28.7 percentage points above the policy rate. This wide spread reflects banks' risk perception (NPL expectations, collateral costs) and capital adequacy buffers.

Credit growth is 36.32% nominal; in real terms (with annual inflation at 29.73%) it is positive at around 29.73-6 but slowing. M3 money supply growth is 28.65%, below inflation — real money supply is contracting. From the perspective of the Friedman rule (money supply growth = real growth + target inflation), this shows the CBRT's monetary policy is on the "tight" side. Credit channel: high credit costs -> suppress consumer demand for durable goods and housing; raise corporate investment financing costs -> delay capacity expansion -> limit future potential growth. However, selective credit policies (exporter, technology, green transition loans) partially offset this pressure.

Domestic demand channel: positive real interest rates -> incentive to postpone consumption -> slowdown in retail sales (annual 10.1% still strong but normalizing with monthly 1.4%). Housing sales and automotive data are not in the dataset; qualitatively: housing loan costs are high, demand shifts toward rentals; in autos, price increases and credit costs suppress demand. Corporate sales: for export-oriented firms, exchange rate advantages and external demand (weak EU growth) are offsetting; for domestic-market-focused firms, weakening demand creates margin pressure.

Liquidity management: The CBRT controls market liquidity through weekly repo and TLREF operations; banks' use of the central bank window (not in the dataset, qualitative) should be trading near the upper bound of the policy rate corridor. Foreign exchange reserves are $62.15 billion (previous $64.57 billion) — the decline in net reserves may stem from FX-protected deposit sales and/or external debt payments. This can also be read as part of liquidity tightening: reserve decline -> withdrawal of TL liquidity -> overnight rates staying close to the policy rate.

In summary: the chain of policy rate -> deposit/loan costs -> domestic demand -> corporate sales/margins is working. The real slowdown in credit growth is the most concrete evidence that the "demand channel" of disinflation is functioning. Risk: if credit contraction is overdosed (e.g., tightening in SME financing), potential output damage could become permanent.

The VIX level of 17.43 is below 2026 averages and signals a "low volatility / high risk appetite" regime. The strengthening DXY combines with rising long-end US Treasury yields (steepening): the market is pricing that Fed rate cuts will be delayed or slower, but it is not expecting a recession — a "no landing" or "soft landing" scenario prevails. In this environment, there is a two-way effect on emerging market (EM) assets: (1) risk appetite is open -> portfolio flows are positive; (2) US real rates staying high -> pressure for EM asset risk premiums (spreads) to widen.

Specific for Turkey: CDS (not in the dataset) and EMBI spreads move in correlation with Fed rate expectations and local disinflation data. In October data, the decline in CPI during the year (31.51% -> 29.73%) and the CBRT's 100 bp cut should have compressed the local risk premium. However, the strengthening DXY and rising US 5-10 year yields could erode the "carry" (interest rate differential) advantage in foreign investors' TL-denominated return calculations: nominal rate differential 36% - 4% = 32%; after subtracting expected TL depreciation (~26% annual inflation differential), real carry is still around ~3.7% — attractive but with volatility risk.

The Fed's "data-dependent" stance is tied to upcoming quarterly CPI/PCE and NFP data. If US inflation plateaus (core PCE does not fall below 3.0%), the Fed may delay cuts -> DXY strengthens further, EM outflow risk rises. If US unemployment rises above 4%, the Fed could shift to "rapid cut" mode -> DXY weakens, flows go to EM. Critical thresholds Turkey should monitor: US core PCE below 0.2% monthly, NFP below 100k, VIX above 17.43.

Investment grade credit spreads (IG) should have narrowed (high risk appetite), and high yield (HY) spreads have also tightened; however, since Turkey is in the HY category, any widening in global HY spreads (e.g., from an energy price shock or a China growth breakdown) directly affects Turkey's spread. Gold holding at $4.074/oz shows central bank buying power continues and real rate expectations have not fully declined yet.

7. Transmission Mechanisms: From Macro to Market

I am building the steps by linking three main chains to the observations in the dataset:

Chain 1: Policy Rate -> Deposit/Loan Cost -> Domestic Demand -> Company Sales -> Margins -> Valuation

  • CBRT policy rate 36.0% -> Deposit rate 43.04% (bank margin ~64.74%) -> Consumer loan rate 64.74% (total margin ~28.7%).

  • High loan cost -> suppresses demand for housing, automotive, durable consumer loans. Retail sales grow 10.1% annually but slow to 1.4% monthly — this means the demand channel is working.

  • On the corporate side: SME loan cost is high (not separately in the dataset, consumer loan rate is a proxy), capacity expansion slows as investment spending is postponed (industrial production -1.86% annually).

  • Sales growth slows, costs (labor, imported inputs, financing) still rising -> operating margins (EBITDA margin) get squeezed. The nearly flat course of the BIST 30 index (13.934.73, -0.10%) shows margin pressure and the increase in discount rate (risk premium) are being priced in.

  • Valuation: High real rates -> WACC (weighted average cost of capital) in DCF models rises -> net present value (NPV) falls. Except for banks (below), valuation multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA) are contracting for most sectors.

Chain 2: Exchange Rate Channel -> Imported Input Cost -> Inflation

  • USD/TRY 49.78 (monthly +0.09%), EUR/TRY 55.48 (monthly -0.62%). REER 105.45 (increase).

  • Imports 31.21 billion USD, exports 25.98 billion USD -> trade deficit 5.23 billion USD.

  • Oil 109.47 USD (decline), but oil price in TL = 109.47 * 49.78 ≈ 5.450 TL/barrel (excluding tax). Previous month 115.81 * 49.74 ≈ 5.760 TL/barrel. ~5.4% decline in TL terms -> decline in PPI energy subgroup (PPI monthly 2.07%, previous 2.57%).

  • However, the REER increase (105.0 -> 105.45) shows the TL's real depreciation continues: imported machinery, raw materials, intermediate goods costs keep rising in TL terms -> producer cost pressure -> pass-through to CPI (delayed, partial).

  • Exchange rate channel: The CBRT has no exchange rate targeting, but liquidity tightening and reserve usage (62.15 billion USD) limit exchange rate volatility. If reserves fall below 60 billion USD, exchange rate passivity increases -> inflation expectations kick in.

Chain 3: Global Rates -> Foreign Capital Flow -> Risk Premium

  • Fed rate 4.0%, 1-year projection 4.1%. US 5Y 4.84%, 30Y 5.30%.

  • CBRT rate 36.0% -> nominal carry ~32%. Expected TL depreciation (annual inflation differential ~26%) -> real carry ~29.73%.

  • VIX 17.43 (low), DXY 102.31 (strong). Risk appetite is open -> foreign portfolio flow (not in the dataset) is probably mildly positive/neutral.

  • Risk premium: Turkey 5Y CDS (not in the dataset) can be estimated at ~300-350 bp levels (qualitative). If the Fed delays cuts -> US 5Y above 4% -> carry narrows -> foreign selling -> TL weakens -> CBRT delays (or raises) rate cuts -> domestic demand is further suppressed. This is an "external shock - internal tightening" loop.

These three chains feed each other: High interest rates -> domestic demand weakens (growth falls) -> current account balance improves (imports fall) -> currency pressure eases -> inflation falls -> opens room for rate cuts. But the budget deficit breaks this cycle: low public savings -> limited current account improvement -> external financing needed -> risk premium stays high -> rate cuts are delayed.

8. Reflections on BIST 30: Sector Channels

The BIST 30 index is at 13.934.73 points, monthly -0.10%; BIST 100 at 11.151.03, -0.01%. High interest rates, weakening domestic demand, currency dynamics and global risk appetite affect sectors differently:

Banking (Weight: ~36-40%): Net interest margin (NIM) expands in a high real interest rate environment (assets: loans 64.74% consumer, commercial loans similar; liabilities: deposits 43.04%). However: (1) Loan growth is contracting in real terms -> volume growth is slowing. (2) NPL (bad debt) risk: delinquencies may rise in the SME and consumer segment (no NPL in the data set). (3) CMB (Capital Adequacy) buffers are strong (BRSA macroprudential tools). Banks are the winning side in a high interest rate environment; but growth momentum is low, valuations (P/B ~1.0-1.2) are still conservative.

Holdings (Koç, Sabancı, İş Bankası etc..): Portfolio effect: bank profits rise, while industrial/energy/automotive segments face margin pressure. NAV (net asset value) discount (holding discount) is still at 30-40% levels. High interest rates -> holding financing costs rise (borrowing costs), but the share of bank profits grows. Net effect: neutral/slightly positive but liquidity and market depth focus on bank stocks.

Industry / Exporters (Automotive, White Goods, Steel, Chemicals): Currency advantage (USD/TRY 49.78, REER 105.45) supports margins; however, EU growth is weak (annual 1.3%, industrial production -1.1%) -> external demand is weak. Imported raw material costs (eased by the oil decline but other commodities are not in the data set) and financing costs are high. Mixed: If global demand recovers (PMI above 50) outperform; otherwise margin squeeze.

Retail / Consumption (Grocers, Textiles, Durable Goods): Domestic demand is slowing (retail sales monthly 1.4%, consumer confidence 84.8). High credit costs -> durable demand is under pressure. Food/retail (BİM, MİGROS) have inelastic demand, strong margin protection -> resilient. Durable goods (ARÇELİK, TOFAŞ) -> negative.

Energy / Oil Refining (TÜPRAŞ): Brent 109.47 USD, crack margins (not in the data set) are critical. TL depreciation (REER increase) supports refinery margins in TL terms; however, weakening domestic demand (gasoline/diesel sales) pressures volumes. Neutral/slightly positive.

Construction / Cement / Iron-Steel: Housing demand depends on credit costs; TOKİ and low-income housing projects (not in the dataset) could provide support. High costs (iron, cement, labor) -> margin pressure.Negative/Selective.

Technology / Software / Defense (ASELSAN, THYAO - aviation): Export-oriented, revenue in dollars, costs in TL. FX advantage + global demand (defense structural increase) -> Positive. THYAO: falling fuel costs, passenger demand (tourism revenues Q2 $15.9 billion) supportive.

Summary: In a high-interest environment Banks > Defense/Technology/Export Holding Companies > Energy > Retail (Food) > Industry (Domestic Market) > Construction/Durable Consumption hierarchy is in play. BIST 30's bank-heavy structure ensures index resilience; however, for the index's "re-rating" (multiple expansion): (1) start of rate cuts, (2) inflation falling below 29.73%, (3) budget discipline signals are required.

9. Scenarios and Monitoring Framework

Three scenarios, conditioned on the possible paths of the variables in the dataset:

Base Scenario (Probability ~55%): Gradual Disinflation and Selective Growth

  • Inflation: CPI 2027 Q1 29.73-27, year-end 27.38-20; PPI declines before CPI.

  • Growth: 2026 year-end 2.5-3.0%; 2027 3.0-3.5% (below potential).

  • Current balance: annual deficit ~$10-15 billion (energy price $100-110/barrel, tourism $60 billion+).

  • Budget: deficit 3.6-7% of GDP; financing from the market, with indirect CBRT support.

  • FX: USD/TRY 49,782 Q1 52-54 band (real depreciation slows).

  • BIST: Banks outperform; index return 15-20% in TL terms, 5-10% in USD terms.

  • Invalidation: If CPI stays above 2.5% monthly (services inflation sticks) -> CBRT pauses/hikes. If Fed holds at 4.0% and DXY above 105 -> portfolio outflows.

  • CBRT: December 36%, January 31% -> real rate still 5%+.

  • Global: Fed March 2027 4% -> DXY below 100 -> EM flows strengthen.

  • Budget: Tax reform (VAT, SCT expansion) -> revenue/GDP up -> deficit falls to 4-5%.
