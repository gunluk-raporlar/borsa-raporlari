1. Global Macroeconomic Outlook

1.1 US: Growth, Inflation and Fed Policy

The US economy recorded strong momentum with 3.4% quarterly growth in the second quarter of 2026, while annual growth stood at 2.1%. Inflation remained at 3.7% on an annual basis with the September 2026 data, with core inflation at 2.6% and core PCE at 3.1%, running above target. The Fed keeps its policy rate at 4.0%, leaving the real rate approximately +0.30 percentage points in positive territory. The unemployment rate was low at 4.1% in October 2026, and wage growth followed a path consistent with inflation at 3.1% annually.

1.2 Eurozone: Stagnation and the ECB's Delicate Balance

The Eurozone posted 0.4% quarterly growth in the third quarter of 2026, while annual growth remained weak at 1.3% (second quarter data). Inflation rose to 4.0% annually in October 2026, while core inflation appeared more controlled at 2.5%. The ECB keeps its policy rate at -1.35%, leaving the real rate approximately -1.35 percentage points in negative territory. Industrial production fell by -0.5% monthly and retail sales by -0.7%, signaling weakening domestic demand. The unemployment rate remains structurally high at 6.5%.

1.3 Global Liquidity and Risk Appetite Indicators

The DXY index at 102.27 reflects the dollar's strength, while the VIX at 19.53 points to relatively calm risk appetite. US Treasury yields — 3-month at 4.12%, 5-year at 4.81%, 30-year at 5.35% — indicate a steepening yield curve. In the Fed's second-quarter 2026 projections, rate expectations of 3.6% for 1 year and 3.4% for 2 years reveal that markets are pricing in a gradual easing scenario. Brent oil at 91.85 USD/barrel implies that inflation pressure via energy costs may persist.

2. Turkey's Macroeconomic Outlook

2.1 Inflation Dynamics and the Disinflation Phase

In Turkey, October 2026 annual consumer inflation was 29.77%, core inflation 28.3%, and PPI inflation 27.41%. Monthly consumer inflation was 2.58%, while monthly core and PPI increases remained at lower levels respectively, indicating that the disinflation process is in the "slowing" phase and has not yet moved to the "sustainable decline" phase. The real policy rate is approximately +6.23 percentage points (policy rate 36% minus CPI 29.77%), maintaining a tight monetary policy stance. Bringing monthly increases down to the 1-1.5% band is critical for anchoring inflation expectations.

2.2 Growth, Unemployment and Capacity Utilization

According to second-quarter 2026 data, GDP grew by 1.1% quarterly and 2.3% annually; this points to below-potential growth due to suppressed domestic demand and limited net export contribution. In September 2026, the unemployment rate remained at 8.5% and the labor force participation rate at 52.7%. Industrial production signaled a recovery with a 2.1% monthly increase in September, and with 2.61% annual growth, the capacity utilization rate rose to 74.5% in October. Retail sales rose by 1.4% monthly and 10.1% annually in August, proving the resilience of consumption.

2.3 Monetary Policy Determination and Real Interest

At its October 2026 meeting, the CBRT kept the policy rate at 36%, demonstrating its determination to maintain positive real interest rates. Deposit rates stood at 42.75% in October and consumer loan rates at 66.46% in September, confirming that the tightening policy continues to transmit to the real economy through banking intermediation. M3 money supply grew 28.85% annually, with nominal growth remaining below inflation, and the contraction in real money supply supports the disinflation process. Although credit growth remains high at 35.29% annually (September), it shows a slowing trend in real terms.

3. Domestic Demand, Credit and Liquidity

3.1 Credit Channel and Interest Rate Transmission

The transmission channel from the policy rate (36%) to bank deposit rates (42.75%) and consumer loan rates (66.46%) shows that financial instruments operate above the policy rate due to risk premiums and operational costs. This structure also pushes up commercial loan costs, suppressing investment and durable consumption spending. The annual credit growth of 35.29% indicates that nominal growth remains high despite positive real rates, but has slowed in real terms.

3.2 Money Supply Growth and Liquidity Conditions

The annual growth of M3 money supply at 28.85% (October 2026) remains below inflation (29.77% annually), indicating a contraction in real money supply. This development is evidence that the CBRT is trying to control market liquidity through liquidity management and reserve options. Banks' recourse to CBRT windows and capital adequacy confirm that credit supply is constrained through price rather than quantity.

3.3 Consumer and Commercial Loan Costs

The consumer loan rate of 66.46% directly affects durable consumption items such as housing and vehicle financing, altering the inter-component structure of domestic demand. Similar cost pressure in commercial loans increases SMEs' working capital financing costs, causing the capacity utilization rate (74.5%) to remain below potential. High real costs trigger companies' decisions to delay investment, constraining potential growth in the medium term.

4. Global Risk Appetite and Interest Rate Expectations

4.1 US Treasury Yields and the Yield Curve

US 3-month Treasury yields at 4.12%, 5-year at 4.81%, and 30-year at 5.35% indicate a bear steepening at the long end of the yield curve. This structure suggests markets are pricing that the Fed will keep the policy rate (4.0%) for longer or slow the pace of cuts, while also pointing to rising long-term inflation premiums and end-of-period borrowing demand. The elevated level of real 10-year yields (inferred from 5-year data) forms the floor for the global cost of capital.

4.2 DXY, VIX and Foreign Capital Flows

The DXY level of 102.27 shows the dollar holding firm against a broad basket of currencies, increasing selling pressure on emerging market (EM) assets. The VIX at 19.53 indicates that "cautious optimism" rather than "fear" prevails in equity markets. However, the combination of a rising DXY and higher US real yields may lead foreign portfolio investors to lighten positions in high-beta markets like Turkey in their risk/reward calculations.

4.3 Fed Projections and Market Pricing

According to the Fed's June 2026 dot plot data, interest rate expectations of 3.6% one year ahead and 3.4% two years ahead reveal that markets base their scenarios on gradual cuts toward 2027. If US inflation (3.7% annually, core PCE 3.1%) proves sticky, these projections could be revised upward, providing additional support to the DXY. For Turkey, this creates an external environment that makes it harder to maintain the carry interest rate differential (TR real rate ~+6.23pp vs US real rate ~+0.30pp).

5. Budget and External Balance

5.1 Budget Performance and Financing Needs

In September 2026, the central government budget posted a deficit of 532 billion TL, indicating that the year-to-date cumulative deficit is widening and financing needs are increasing. The budget deficit-to-GDP ratio (based on the annual GDP estimate) may fluctuate in the 3.6-6% band; this level implies that, given insufficient domestic savings, external financing or indirect central bank financing (which is limited under this policy framework) may be needed. On the revenue side, tax elasticity, and on the expenditure side, interest payments and personnel costs are the main dynamics.

4,053 Current Account Balance, Trade and Tourism Revenues

In the third quarter of 2026, the current account posted a surplus of 4,053 billion USD, marking the first quarterly surplus in 12 years. However, in October, the trade balance recorded a deficit of 6.7 billion USD (exports 25.9 billion USD, imports 32.58 billion USD). Tourism revenues provided a strong buffer at 25.53 billion USD in Q3. The sustainability of the current account surplus remains fragile depending on energy bills (Brent 91.85 USD) and the course of gold imports.

56.09 Foreign Exchange Reserves and External Financing Vulnerability

As of the week of November 5, 2026, the central bank's gross foreign exchange reserves stood at 56.09 billion USD, with net reserves estimated at lower levels. The decline in reserves in recent weeks (from 59.04 billion USD previously) indicates that the current account surplus is not fully reflected in reserve accumulation, suggesting portfolio outflows or bank-related usage of facilities. The short-term external debt stock and banks' rollover ratios for facilities are the main monitoring points for external financing vulnerability.

6. Energy, Commodities, Foreign Exchange and Bond Markets

6.1 Oil Prices and Inflation Transmission

Brent oil at 91.85 USD/barrel and WTI at 86.28 USD/barrel keep the cost-push inflation pressure on Turkey's PPI and therefore CPI via imported input costs alive. Every 10 USD increase in oil prices, netting out the exchange rate effect, can contribute approximately 0.5-0.7 points to annual inflation. OPEC+ supply discipline and global demand expectations keep this risk tilted to the upside.

6.2 Gold, Exchange Rates and Real Effective Exchange Rate

Gold is trading at 4.038 USD per ounce, gram gold at 6.521 TRY, while USD/TRY at 50.23 and EUR/TRY at 56.07 show relatively controlled exchange rate movements. The Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) rose to 107.76 index points (2003=100) in October 2026, indicating that the real appreciation of the TRY continues and, despite the improvement in the current account balance, external competitiveness is weakening. Exchange rate appreciation suppresses imported inflation but erodes exporter margins.

6.3 Bond Yields and Local Market Dynamics

In the local bond market, the policy rate (36%) and inflation expectations determine yields. Although high real interest rates (approximately +6.23pp) keep the carry trade attractive for foreign investors, exchange rate risk (REER level) and political uncertainties limit these flows. Banks' Treasury bond portfolios and TRY liquidity management ensure that the yield curve trades anchored to the policy rate at the short end and to inflation expectations at the long end.

7. Transmission Mechanisms: From Macro to Market

7.1 Interest Rate Channel: From Policy Rate to Company Valuations

Policy rate (36%) -> Bank deposit/loan costs (42.75% / 66.46%) -> Increase in corporate borrowing costs -> Postponement of company investment expenditures -> Slower sales growth -> Margin pressure (interest expenses/revenue) -> Higher discount rate in DCF models -> Compression of valuation multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA). This chain creates a dampening effect, especially in highly leveraged and investment-intensive sectors (holdings, industry).

7.2 Exchange Rate Channel: Imported Input Costs and Inflation

USD/TRY (50.23) and EUR/TRY (56.07) -> Imported raw material/intermediate goods costs (Oil 91.85 USD, Gold 4.038 USD) -> PPI (annual 27.41%) -> CPI (annual 29.77%) -> Erosion of real wages -> Decline in consumer purchasing power -> Volumetric slowdown in retail sales. The REER level of 107.76 shows that exchange rate pass-through is still high and that supporting disinflation through the exchange rate channel is difficult.

7.3 Capital Flow Channel: Global Rates and Risk Premium

US 5Y yield (4.81%) + Turkey risk premium (CDS/EMBI estimate) -> Local bond yield expectation -> Foreign portfolio demand (BIST, Treasury bonds). The rise in DXY (102.27) and expectations of delayed Fed cuts widen the risk premium, putting foreign flows into "wait-and-see" mode. The CBRT's net reserve position (56 billion USD gross) and current account surplus (4 billion USD) act as a buffer against shocks in this channel, but adequacy is being tested at the limit.

8. Reflections on BIST 30: Sector Channels

8.1 Banking: Interest Margin and Credit Risk Balance

Banks protect margins on the asset side through repricing of loan rates (commercial/credit cards), while on the liability side deposit costs (42.75%) remain high. The net interest margin (NIM) may expand nominally but carries a risk of contraction in real terms. The high interest rate environment can increase credit risk costs (NPL ratio, Stage 2 transitions) and limit profitability. The heavy weight of banks in BIST 30 increases the index's sensitivity to interest rates.

8.2 Industry and Holdings: Exchange Rate and Input Cost Sensitivity

Industrial and holding companies are under cost pressure due to their high share of imported inputs (oil, metals, chemicals) and the USD/TRY level (50.23). With REER at 107.76, exchange rate appreciation accelerates the increase in dollar-denominated costs in TRY terms. Capacity utilization at 74.5% limits pricing power. The holding structure can distribute risk through cash flow transfers among subsidiaries, but consolidated debt/equity ratios should be monitored.

8.3 Retail and Consumption: Domestic Demand Sensitivity

Although retail sales grew 10.1% year-on-year in August, real wages remaining below inflation (annual 29.77%) and the consumer confidence index (84.8 points) staying in the pessimistic zone (below 100) carry the risk of volumetric slowdown in coming quarters. Consumer loan rates at 66.46% constrain demand for durable goods (white goods, automotive). While companies try to pass cost increases into prices to protect margins, demand elasticity partially blocks this pass-through.

9. Scenarios and Monitoring Framework

9.1 Base Scenario: Gradual Softening

As inflation runs in the 2-2.5% monthly band, it slows on an annual basis (year-end 25-26% may be targeted), and the CBRT begins gradual cuts of 250-300 bp starting in the first quarter of 2027. Growth is pulled below potential (3-3.5%), and the current account imbalance remains manageable. In this scenario, BIST 30 income-focused, low-beta stocks (non-bank financials, consumption) gain relative outperformance.

9.2 Optimistic Scenario: Rapid Disinflation and Early Rate Cut

If an unexpected decline in food and energy prices (Brent below 80 USD), a rapid fall in food inflation, and TL appreciation (USD/TRY below 50.228) combine, annual inflation could fall to 22-23% by year-end. The CBRT could make a 500 bp front-loaded cut in late 2026/early 2027. In this case, real rates would remain positive, the credit channel would revive, BIST beta would rise at 4.8, and foreign inflows would accelerate if global risk appetite (VIX <4.8, DXY <100) supports.

9.3 Pessimistic Scenario: Inflation Deviation and External Shock

In a scenario with Brent above 100 USD, geopolitical risk (Hormuz, Ukraine), and a sudden TL depreciation (USD/TRY above 50.228), inflation could stay above 29.77% by year-end. The CBRT may be forced to hike rates (40-36%). Higher real rates would push domestic demand into a deep recession, the budget deficit would balloon with interest expenses, and the current account balance would deteriorate. Reserve losses would accelerate, BIST 30 would see a 20%+ correction in dollar terms, and bank NPLs would rise.

9.4 Critical Monitoring Indicators and Triggers

1) Monthly CPI/PPI (target: sustainable <1.5%), 2) Central Bank net reserves and swap usage, 3) US CPI/PCE and Fed dot plot updates, 4) Brent oil and DXY trend, 5) Monthly budget cash realizations and borrowing calendar, 6) Bank credit growth in real terms (below 4% annual), 7) REER pulled below 105. Any deviation of these indicators from the base scenario requires updating probability weights.

10. Conclusion and Evaluation

10.1 General Assessment

The Turkish economy is in the "slowdown" phase on the disinflation path with high real rates (+6.23pp) and tight monetary policy; for inflation to fall to sustainable single-digit levels, monthly increases need to lock into the 1-1.5% band and expectations need to be anchored. The global environment creates headwinds for emerging market assets with the Fed's "higher for longer" rhetoric and a strong dollar (DXY 102.27). Although the external balance (current account surplus of 4 billion USD, tourism 25.5 billion USD) delivers positive surprises, reserve adequacy and budget discipline remain risk factors.

10.2 Risk Balances

Upside risks: Energy prices, food supply shocks, stronger-than-expected exchange rate pass-through, and financing of the budget deficit through indirect central bank channels. Downside risks: Global recession (EU industry -1.1% YoY) weakens export demand, suppressed domestic demand locks growth below potential, and financial stability tightens the risk premium. The base scenario assumes these risks are managed in a balanced way, but the margin of error is narrow.

Methodology and Terms

  • Signal (general): Stock-based general technical evaluation (STRONG BUY / BUY / NEUTRAL / SELL / STRONG SELL); seen on the board and in the technical screening table.
  • Oscillator vote: Individual BUY/SELL votes of indicators such as RSI, StochK, MACD, CCI, WT; given in the format "11 buy/8 sell" and may conflict with the general signal (for example, a stock may diverge positively in oscillator votes while the board signal is SELL).
  • WT (Wave Trend): short-term cycle (peak/trough) oscillator; generates BUY/SELL signals.
  • Channel (%N): position of the price within the 60-day regression channel; 0% near the channel bottom (trough area), 100% near the channel top (peak area).
  • ADX: Average Directional Index; values above 25 indicate a strong trend, values below 20 indicate weak/sideways movement.
  • r: Pearson correlation coefficient calculated over a 60-day window (trend strength indicator); given in parentheses in the model portfolio table.
  • CASH: the portion not allocated to stocks in the model portfolio, held for protection.
