1. Global Macroeconomic Outlook

ABD: Strong Growth and Sticky Inflation

The ABD economy recorded annual growth of 2.1% and quarterly growth of 3.4% in the second quarter of 2026, demonstrating resilience beyond its peers. This performance was driven by a 3.2% quarterly increase in consumer spending and support for private investments; the labor market remained tight with an unemployment rate of 4.1% in October data and an employment increase of 165 thousand people. On the inflation front, headline CPI at an annual 3.7% and core CPI at 2.6% remain above the Fed's 2% target, PCE indexes also (headline 3.6%, core 3.1%) signal similar stickiness. The Fed keeps the policy rate at 4.0%, maintaining the "higher for longer" narrative; dot plot projections signal an average rate expectation of 3.6% for next year and 3.4% for two years, supporting a gradual easing scenario.

Euro Area: Stagnation Risk and Negative Real Interest Rate

On the European Union side, the picture is clearly weaker; with quarterly growth of 0.4% in the third quarter, the risk of technical stagnation continues, industrial production's annual decline of 1.1% confirms a manufacturing pullback. Inflation in October data came in at headline 4.0%, core 2.5%; while the ECB policy rate stays at 2.65%, the real policy rate is around -1.30 points (calculated: 2.65% - 4.0%) in negative territory. This negative real rate keeps borrowing costs cheap in real terms, exhibiting a demand‑biased stance; however, the 0.4% annual growth in retail sales and consumer confidence remaining at 4.8 points indicate that private consumption has not yet revived. The current account balance posted a surplus of 7.7 milyar avro in August data, while the trade balance showed a deficit of -16.1 milyar avro, reminding of structural external trade weakness driven by energy imports.

Global Liquidity and Risk Appetite

The DXY index's rise to 102.30 and the increase in US 10‑year bond yields (5‑year 4.65%, 30‑year 5.26%) signal a tightening of global dollar liquidity and a rise in risk premiums due to end‑of‑period portfolio balances. Although the VIX's retreat to 25.64 (previous 28.18) signals short‑term relief, its staying above the 20 level proves that market participants are still pricing in an uncertainty premium. In this environment, for emerging market assets (EM), the positive US real rate (0.30%) and the uncertainty of the Fed's easing roadmap create a selective and fragile structure in capital flows.

2. Turkey's Macroeconomic Outlook

Current Phase of Disinflation

According to October 2026 data, the Turkish Economy is in the "mid‑point" phase of the disinflation process with an annual TÜFE inflation of 29.77%; the monthly increase of 2.58% (annualized ~2.58%) shows that inflation has not yet entered a durable downtrend, and that pricing behaviors and indexation mechanisms are still operating at high levels. Core inflation (excluding groups B and C) stands at an annual 28.3%, while ÜFE is at an annual 27.41% (monthly 1.65%), indicating continued cost‑driven pressures. Although the TCMB lowered the policy rate from 37% to 36% at its October meeting, the calculated real policy rate remains in tight territory at +4.80 points on a TÜFE basis and +6.23 points on a core basis. While this real rate level is sufficient for demand suppression, it requires an extension of the "tight stance" period for inflation expectations to recede.

Growth and Employment Dynamics

Second quarter GDP data reflects the economy's sub‑potential trajectory and a domestic‑demand‑driven slowdown with annual growth of 2.3% (quarterly 1.1%). The monthly -1.0% and annual -1.86% decline in industrial production in August data signals weakening on the production side; while the 10.1% annual increase (monthly 1.4%) in retail sales in August data indicates that nominal consumption still appears strong, it likely contracted in real terms (TÜFE‑adjusted). The unemployment rate stands at 8.5% in September data, the labor force participation rate at 52.7%, while the business confidence index (99.8) and consumer confidence index (84.8) falling below 100 show that future expectations have become fragile and the savings rate is trending upward.

Leading Indicators and Confidence Channels

Economic Confidence Index (97.3) and Capacity Utilization Rate (74.5%) data reveal that industry has entered a demand wait-and-see mode and that investment decision-making processes have lengthened. While exports remained flat at the $25.9 billion level in October, imports rose to $32.58 billion, pushing the trade deficit to $6.7 billion. This structure, supported by the rise of the real effective exchange rate index (REER) to 107.76 in October (previous 105.1), indicates that the "strong currency" regime suppresses imported input costs while weakening export competitiveness. Tourism revenues reaching $25.53 billion in the third quarter continues to play a critical buffer role in financing the current account.

3. Domestic Demand, Credit and Liquidity

Credit-Money Supply Gap and Transmission Lag

According to September data, the annual 35.29% growth of bank loans significantly exceeds the annual 28.85% growth of M3 money supply (October data). This "credit-fast, money-slow" structure shows that the wide interest margin resulting from banks' deposit collection costs (deposit rate 42.75%) reflecting onto loan rates (consumer loan 66.46%) encourages credit supply; however, the real sector's willingness to use this costly source has broken in interest-sensitive investment items (housing, durable consumption). Credit growing faster than money supply suggests that banks have activated liquidity usage from Central Bank windows (lender of last resort) or external borrowing channels; this creates a "leakage" channel that delays the effectiveness of liquidity tightening policy.

Interest Rate Channels: Deposit and Credit Costs

The transmission channel from the policy rate (36%) to the deposit rate (42.75%) and consumer loan rate (66.46%) clearly reveals that banks demand a liquidity premium and a credit risk premium. The high deposit rate encourages households to keep (or convert) their savings in TL, suppressing dollarization; however, the consumer loan cost at the 36% level has nearly frozen housing and vehicle purchases, implying that much of the nominal growth in retail sales is driven by mandatory consumption (food, energy) and inflation effects. Commercial loan rates for firms (not in data, qualitative) are likely below consumer loans; nevertheless, positive real interest rates create the risk of return on invested capital (ROIC) falling below the cost of financing.

Effects on Domestic Demand

The high real interest rate environment pulls up the household savings rate by postponing durable consumption and housing investments; this leads to a structural softening in aggregate demand through the "paradox of thrift" mechanism. Considering that the gap between retail sales growth (10.1% y/y) and inflation (29.77% y/y) in the data shows that real retail sales have collapsed (annual ~-29.77% levels), it is seen that disinflation has begun to operate through the domestic demand channel, but this process is occurring at the cost of tightening in the labor market (unemployment 8.5%). The slight slowdown in credit growth (from previous 36.32% to 35.29%) is an early sign that policy is starting to take effect through the credit channel.

4. Global Risk Appetite and Interest Rate Expectations

Fed Projections and Market Pricing

According to the Fed's June 2026 dot plot data, the policy rate is projected to be lowered to 3.6% one year ahead and 3.4% two years ahead. The alignment of the current policy rate (4.0%) and the 3-month Treasury yield (4.0%) indicates that markets have priced in arrival at the "terminal rate" point; however, the rise in 5-year (4.65%) and 30-year (5.26%) yields (bear steepening) shows that the long-term inflation premium and borrowing supply are being priced. This curve structure narrows the valuation margins of long-term assets (equities, long-term bonds) while directing banks' carry trade toward shorter-term instruments.

DXY, VIX and Yield Curve

The DXY index's rise to the 102.30 level (+0.74%) proves that dollar demand remains structurally strong due to the Fed's "higher for longer" rhetoric and the EU/US growth gap (US 3.4% quarterly vs EU 0.4%). Although VIX's retreat to 25.64 (-9%) signals a short-term "risk-on" correction, its failure to fall below the 20-25 band shows that market participants are still pricing protection costs (put options) due to upcoming data dependency (NFP, CPI, Fed speeches). The US 30-year Treasury yield at 5.26% reignites the "high neutral rate" (r*) debate dating back to pre-2007 periods.

Reflection on Emerging Markets

Global liquidity tightening and the positive trajectory of US real interest rates (0.30%) strengthen "flight to quality" dynamics in emerging market portfolio flows. For Turkey, this environment means that foreign investors demand a higher risk premium (CDS, EMBI spread - not in data, qualitative) for entry into BIST. However, the CBRT's tight monetary policy (real interest rate +4.80%) and the current account's surprise surplus (+4 bn $) push Turkey into a "relatively safe harbor" position within the "high yield, high risk" pool, allowing foreign capital to enter selectively (especially TL time deposits and short-term government bonds).

5. Budget and External Balance

Budget Deficit and Interest Expenditures

September central budget data shows a 532 billion TL deficit (12.9 billion TL more than previous month), indicating that seasonal and structural factors moved together in the ninth month of the year. The main driver of this deficit is the interest expenditure item exploding as a result of the high policy rate (36%) and the rollover of the debt stock (no detail in data, qualitative). On the revenue side, the nominal growth of inflation-indexed taxes (VAT, SCT) cannot fully offset the interest shock on the expenditure side. Although the budget deficit-to-GDP ratio (nominal GDP value not in data, cannot be calculated) remains at manageable levels, it is clear that interest expenditures have locked the budget into a "mandatory expenditure" cage and that primary balance (excl. interest) targets are at risk.

Current Account: Tourism and Energy Factor

The current account balance for the August period (quarterly) posting a surplus of 4,053 billion dollars (estimate 3.6 Bn $) is a strong signal that the structural deficit in the 12-month cumulative data is closing. The engine of this surplus is tourism revenue reaching 25.53 billion dollars in the third quarter; despite the limited increase in tourist numbers (0.4%), the increase in per capita spending and the exchange rate advantage (REER 107.76) supported real tourism revenue. However, the trade balance posting a 6.7 billion dollar deficit in October (imports 32.58 Bn $ vs exports 25.9 Bn $) reminds that energy prices (Brent 98.32 $) and gold imports (data not available, qualitative) are the main risk factors for the structural sustainability of the current account.

Reserves and External Financing Fragility

The decline of the Central Bank's gross foreign exchange reserves to 56.09 billion dollars (previous 59.04 Bn $) shows that the net reserve position (excluding swaps - data not available) is still tight and that the Central Bank follows a reserve accumulation strategy (encouraging carry trade entries) when market conditions permit. The short-term external debt stock (data not available) and banks' foreign borrowing rollover rates continue to be the main determinants of external financing fragility. The realization of the current surplus through \"temporary\" tourism revenue brings with it the risk that foreign direct investment (FDI - data not available) and portfolio flows on the financing side are not stable.

6. Energy, Commodities, Foreign Exchange and Bond Markets

Oil and Inflation Import

Brent oil trading at 98.32 dollars/barrel (WTI 90.83 $) shows that the balance of OPEC+ supply discipline and global demand expectations maintains a \"high price\" equilibrium point. For Turkey, the oil bill (annual ~45-50 Bn $ estimate, data not available) is the largest item of the current account deficit, and Brent staying in the 90-100 $ band creates a continuous \"floor\" for inflation through the PPI (annual 27.41%) and consequently through the CPI (energy weight ~15%-20). This structure forces the Central Bank to revise \"energy assumptions\" in its inflation forecasting models upward, creating a risk of delaying the timing of interest rate cuts.

Gold: Safe Haven and Protection

The international gold price trading at historic peaks at the 4.075 dollar per ounce level shows that central banks' (China, India, Turkey - data not available) reserve diversification habits and the geopolitical risk premium (Middle East, Ukraine) are priced in, despite the low/positive level of real US interest rates. The retreat of gram gold to the 6.569.63 TL level (previous 6.632.82 TL) is the result of the combination of the relatively stable course in the USD/TRY exchange rate (50.15 TL) and the decline in global gold prices (-0.94%). In a high inflation environment, gold continues to be in demand by domestic investors as a \"store of value\" and \"exchange rate protection\" instrument even if its real return is negative (storage cost, no yield).

Exchange Rate, REER and Competitiveness

USD/TRY remaining relatively stable at the 50.15 TL level (monthly change -0.01%) reflects the CBRT's \"controlled melt\" (managed float) regime and the locking of market liquidity into the TL through bank deposit interest rates (42.75%). The retreat of EUR/TRY to 55.86 TL (-0.85%) is the result of the Euro's global weakening and the TL's appreciation against the Euro. The critical observation is the Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) index rising to the 107.76 level in October (2003=100 base); this proves that the TL is trading in the \"overvalued\" region, above historical averages in both nominal and real terms. While this overvaluation erodes the exporter's profit margin, it supports the fight against inflation through the \"exchange rate channel\" by keeping imported input costs artificially cheap; however, it creates a \"J-curve\" trap that prevents the structural improvement of the current account (export increase/import decrease).

7. Transmission Mechanisms: From Macro to Market

Interest Rate Channel: From Policy Rate to Company Valuation

The mechanism follows this chain: CBRT Policy Rate (36%) → Bank Deposit Rate (42.75%) / Loan Rate (66.46% consumer, commercial lower) → Increase in Companies' Weighted Average Cost (WACC) → Postponement/Cancellation of Investment Expenditure (CAPEX) → Discounting of Future Cash Flows (FCF) (Higher Discount Rate) → Narrowing of Company Valuation (EV/EBITDA, P/E). High real interest rate (+4.80%), combined with risk premium (Equity Risk Premium), pushes the equity risk premium (Cost of Equity) into the 36%-50 band (data not available, qualitative). In this environment, companies with low leverage, strong cash flow and high pricing power (oligopoly/regulation protected) (Banks, Markets, Some Holdings) maintain valuation protection; while highly leveraged, investment-heavy, weak pricing power companies (Construction, Some Industrial Sub-sectors) fall into a \"value trap\".

Exchange Rate Channel: From Imported Input Cost to Consumer Inflation

USD/TRY (50.15) and REER (107.76) levels show that a \"nominal exchange rate fixing\" policy suppressing imported input costs (PPI 27.41%) is being followed. Mechanism: Nominal Exchange Rate Fixing → Imported Raw Material/R&D/Production Costs Controlled in TL Terms → PPI Suppressed → Pass-through to CPI (excluding Intermediate Goods/Food/Energy) Slows → Core Inflation (28.3%) Slows. This channel is the \"accelerator\" element of disinflation. However, the REER level of 107 questions the sustainability of this process; implying that the exchange rate needs a nominal depreciation (annual ~25%-30) to cover the realized inflation differential (TR 3.7% vs US 3.7% / EU 4.0%), and that the current level represents a \"delayed adjustment\".

Capital Flow Channel: From Global Interest Rate to Risk Premium

Fed Rate (4.0%) / US 10-Year Yield (4.65%) → Global Risk-Free Rate High → Required Return (Hurdle Rate) for Emerging Markets (EM) Increases → Foreign Portfolio Flows (BIST, GDDS) Become Selective / Exit → TL Demand Weakens / CDS Premiums Rise → CBRT Forced to Use Reserves / Raise Rates. The BIST 100's increase of +1.32% in TL terms and +1.33% in USD terms in the data shows that foreign selling (data not available, qualitative) was met by domestic institutional/individual buying (deposit interest arbitrage). This \"domestically fed\" rally has a fragile structure against external shocks (Fed surprise, Oil shock).

8. Reflections on BIST 30: Sector Channels

Banking: Net Interest Margin and Credit Risk

Banks protect their Net Interest Margin (NIM) in a high policy rate environment by benefiting from the time lag in \"asset-liability repricing\"; deposit cost (42.75%) rises more slowly than loan yield (commercial ~50%-55, consumer 66%). However, positive real interest rate (+4.80%) weakens borrowers' payment capacity; the increase in Cost of Risk (CoR) (Stage 2/3 transitions) can erode NIM gains. The performance of the BIST Bank Index depends on the balance between \"interest margin optimism\" and \"credit cost pessimism\"; banks with strong capital adequacy (CAR) and long-term TL asset/liability balance are advantageous.

Exporting Industry: Exchange Rate and Cost Squeeze

Sectors such as Automotive, White Goods, Steel, Chemicals face the dilemma of \"exchange rate pressure\" at the REER 107.76 level and \"energy/raw material cost\" at Brent 98 $. While export prices (USD based) cannot be increased due to weak global demand (EU Industrial Production -1.1%), input costs are under control in TL terms thanks to exchange rate protection; however, margins (EBITDA margin) are narrowing. These sectors are the group that gains the most value (or loses the least) in a TL nominal depreciation (devaluation) scenario due to the operational leverage effect; while in a \"strong currency\" scenario they carry a \"value trap\" risk.

Domestic Market Focused: Consumer and Retail (Markets, Textile, Durable Goods) face a double squeeze due to the erosion of real wages (Inflation 29.77% vs Wage Increase data not available, qualitative but likely real negative) and consumer credit interest rate being at 66.46% level. Markets (BIM, MIGROS) maintain margin protection thanks to the nature of compulsory consumption, pricing power and inventory turnover speed; while durable goods (Arçelik, Vestel - also exporters) and automotive (Tofaş, Ford Otosan - also exporters) are under volume decline and margin pressure because the credit channel is completely blocked. Holding-structured companies (Koç, Sabancı) provide protection thanks to portfolio diversity and bank profits; however, NAV (Net Asset Value) discounts can push share prices below fundamental values.

10. Conclusion and Evaluation

As of November 2026, the Turkish economy is executing a demand-side disinflation engineering through high real interest rates (+4.80%/+6.23); however, this engineering is built on a strong-currency-biased nominal exchange rate regime, with the real effective exchange rate (REER 107.76) trading in historical upper bands. While this structure is effective in breaking inflation expectations and suppressing imported inflation, it means eroding export competitiveness, preventing structural improvement of the current account (excluding tourism), and linking sustainability to the risk of nominal exchange rate correction (devaluation). The weakening of budget discipline under the shock of interest expenditures, the continuation of external financing fragility (reserves 56 Mrd $), and the increase in the new market risk premium due to global liquidity tightening (DXY 102.3, US 30Y 5.26%) place the macroeconomic balance on "thin ice".

For BIST 30, this environment creates a sectoral divergence (dispersion) between "interest margin optimism" (Banks), "exchange rate/cost squeeze pessimism" (Exporting Industry), and "consumption collapse risk" (Retail/Durables). Although the index level (14.290) provides nominal protection on a TL basis, it must close the inflation differential (26%-27) to avoid losing real growth on a USD basis (XU030 USD); this requires either strong currency appreciation (unlikely) or stock returns exceeding inflation (company profits growing nominally 2.3%+). Current macro conditions show that the second scenario (profit growth) is difficult, while the primary scenario (currency correction) carries systemic risk.

Strategic focus for the investor; there should be a "flight to quality" in companies with strong balance sheets (Low Net Debt/EBITDA), resilient cash flow, pricing power, and manageable TL asset/liability balance. Sectorally; capital adequacy and CoR control in banking, market chains in retail, and the potential for closing the NAV discount in holdings stand out. Methodology and Terms

  • Signal (general): Stock-based general technical evaluation (STRONG BUY / BUY / NEUTRAL / SELL / STRONG SELL); seen on the board and in the technical screening table.
  • Oscillator vote: Individual BUY/SELL votes of indicators such as RSI, StochK, MACD, CCI, WT; given in the format "11 buy/8 sell" and may conflict with the general signal (for example, a stock may diverge positively in oscillator votes while the board signal is SELL).
  • WT (Wave Trend): short-term cycle (peak/trough) oscillator; generates BUY/SELL signals.
  • Channel (%N): position of the price within the 60-day regression channel; 0% near the channel bottom (trough area), 100% near the channel top (peak area).
  • ADX: Average Directional Index; values above 25 indicate a strong trend, values below 20 indicate weak/sideways movement.
  • r: Pearson correlation coefficient calculated over a 60-day window (trend strength indicator); given in parentheses in the model portfolio table.
  • CASH: the portion not allocated to stocks in the model portfolio, held for protection.
