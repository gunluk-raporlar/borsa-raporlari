Comity

A term of international cooperation relating to competition policies. In the case of negative comity, each country party to the international cooperation undertakes to take into account the important interests of other countries when applying its own competition law. In the case of positive comity, the relevant authority in country A requests the competition authority of country B to act on anticompetitive practices existing in country B that significantly affect market conditions in country A

General expenses are the costs that arise when an undertaking produces more than one product but cannot be attributed directly to the production of a single product. When production of one of these products ends, the undertaking cannot rid itself of its general expenses. It is possible to divide general expenses into common costs and operating expenses. A common cost is the cost that arises when multiple products are produced in a single production process. For example, crude oil is a common cost for these products, since both fuel oil and gasoline are produced from it

Competition advocacy comprises the initiatives of competition authorities, in addition to enforcing competition rules, essentially through relations with other public institutions and by raising public awareness of the benefits of competition, aimed at establishing a competitive environment and increasing competition. Many activities can be regarded as competition advocacy. For example, giving opinions on current legislation or draft legislation, and on state interventions that could encourage practices restricting competition

Another of the competition-based tests used in the control of concentrations, besides the dominance test, is the 'Substantial Lessening of Competition Test' (SLC Test). The difference between the two tests stems from the difference of approach to the causes of the anticompetitive effects of mergers, rather than the effects themselves. Competitive Price

It is the price formed in a market where there are no market frictions or violations of competition and where supply and demand are freely determined. This price is expected to be close to marginal cost and marginal revenue.

Consumer Welfare

Refers to the individual benefits consumers obtain from the consumption of goods and services. Individual welfare is defined theoretically as the individual assessments individuals make about their own satisfaction. Therefore, for consumer welfare to be measured exactly, individual preferences must be known exactly. In practice, the concept of consumer surplus is used to measure consumer welfare. Total consumer welfare is the consumer surplus for all consumers

The use of the fiber optic infrastructure capacity established in the energy sector also for the provision of telecommunication services is an example of convergence.

DollarizationDollarisation / Currency Substitution

The use by residents of a country of foreign currency instead of their own national currency as a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value. Full dollarization is a country's completely abandoning its national currency and adopting a foreign currency as its official currency. Partial dollarization arises when economic units in a country, in an environment of high inflation and uncertainty, begin to prefer financial assets in foreign currency instead of financial assets in national currency, in order to protect themselves from the possible loss of value of the national currency. It is generally observed that the first stage of dollarization develops as 'asset substitution', the beginning of the use of foreign-currency assets as a store of value. On the other hand, the banking crises frequently experienced in the recent past, particularly in emerging markets, have added to the economics literature the concept of 'liability dollarization' in addition to asset dollarization. Both asset and liability dollarization can be gathered under a single heading and characterized as 'financial dollarization'. Financial dollarization is defined as the tendencies of residents of a country to hold both their assets and their liabilities in foreign currency.

Exchange rate regimes Exchange Rate Regimes

Today, owing to the importance they carry, particularly for small and open economies, and their potential to directly affect the real economy, exchange rates are used directly as target, indicator or instrument within the framework of the monetary policy implemented. Exchange rate practices take shape between two regimes: the fixed exchange rate regime at one end and free floating at the other. Under a fixed exchange rate regime, the value of the national currency is pegged against a foreign currency or a basket of currencies, and the maintenance of this value is guaranteed by the monetary authority, sometimes explicitly and sometimes implicitly. In Currency Board-type arrangements, while the rate is pegged on the one hand, on the other hand the national money supply is tied entirely to the foreign currency purchases and sales conducted at this rate. Under a free floating exchange rate regime, the price of the exchange rate is determined according to the supply and demand forming in the market. Besides these, many intermediate regimes exist. For example, under a floating regime the rate is largely determined by market supply and demand conditions, but the monetary authority intervenes in the market rarely and in limited amounts; under a managed float regime, again there is no exchange rate target, but interventions can occur frequently for various reasons. Under regimes known as the crawling peg, the rate is guided according to a set of criteria and is intervened in by the monetary authority when necessary. The choice of such exchange rate regimes varies according to the conditions countries find themselves in.

Cross Rate Cross Rate

In the international markets, since exchange rates are generally expressed as the rate of exchange between the US dollar and the local currencies, the rate of exchange between two currencies other than the US dollar can be calculated indirectly according to their prices in US dollars. The cross rate is the exchange rate defined as the parity between two foreign currencies and each of these two currencies with a third foreign currency (usually the US dollar). Core Inflation Core Inflation

The permanent component of inflation stripped of temporary or one-off effects. Excluding certain items from the index, statistical analysis and model-based methods are frequently used in calculating core inflation; however, the most widely followed core inflation indicators are those obtained by the exclusion method. For this purpose, energy prices, basic food prices and indirect taxes, which are more exposed to external effects (energy prices, seasonal conditions, fiscal policy, etc.) and can be temporary in nature, are the main elements excluded from such inflation calculations. The purpose of using core inflation is to identify the elements that make the change in the general price level persistent. Although the inflation target is set on headline CPI inflation, the course of core inflation indicators is also taken into account so that more realistic policy decisions can be taken. In particular, in countries moving toward inflation targeting, rather than a single core inflation indicator, alternative indices calculated in different ways are followed, considering the advantages and disadvantages of all calculation methods.

Core Liabilities Core Liabilities

The deposit-based funding that the banking sector obtains from savholders domestically is generally called core liabilities. Since core liabilities grow in proportion to the economy's size and the household's wealth, they are accepted as a source compatible with the economy's fundamentals for banks' credit expansion. In the credit expansion periods in which credits grow faster than deposits, banks can turn to non-core liabilities and thereby feed the macro-financial vulnerabilities in the financial system.

Output Gap Output Gap

The output gap, defined as the difference of the output realized in an economy from its potential level, is an important monetary policy variable in the evaluation of the price pressures in the economy. Under the inflation targeting regime, central banks shape their policies according to the forward-looking inflation expectations rather than realized inflation. Accordingly, in the areas where the output gap is positive, it is estimated that the economy is operating above capacity because of the demand surplus, and that this can cause demand-side inflationary pressure. In contrast, in the areas where the output gap is negative, it is estimated that there is a supply surplus or idle capacity stemming from the weak demand in the economy, and that this too can cause a fall in inflation.

Output market output market

Anchor Nominal Anchor

It is used to express the magnitudes taken as reference in the economic decision-making processes. For example, the concept of the exchange rate anchor expresses that, following a central bank's making certain commitments for the exchange rate, and with the other economic units also trusting this commitment, the changes in the exchange rate are taken as the basis in the economic decisions taken, and the decisions such as price, wage increase and investment take shape within the framework of the exchange rate policy. In the absence of trust in such an anchor (policy), the selected magnitudes lose these properties. The monetary magnitudes, interest rates, inflation and growth rates are the magnitudes used as 'anchors'. Particularly in recent years, the difficulties arising in the use of magnitudes such as the exchange rate and the interest rate as anchors have generalized the use of inflation for this purpose.
