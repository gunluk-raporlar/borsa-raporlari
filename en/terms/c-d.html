cartel cartel

Catch-up effect catch-up effect

Central Bank merkez bankası

It is the public institution that undertakes the task of managing the monetary system of one or more countries.

Central Bank Independence Central Bank Independence

It is the ability of monetary authorities to take institutional, managerial, financial and monetary policy decisions independently of any pressure.

Central Bank Instrument Independence Instrument Independence

Means the central bank's being able to freely choose, without needing the approval of the government or another authority, the monetary policy instruments and methods it will use to reach its final aim set by law, and its being able to use these instruments freely. For central banks, independence mostly expresses instrument independence.

Central Bank Intervention Merkez Bankası Müdahalesi

The making of purchases and sales by central banks in the markets, directed toward realizing their short- and long-term monetary policy aims. With the intervention, the aim is to lower/raise the market prices of the variables intervened in, such as exchange rates and interest, to the levels foreseen in the policy. Intervention methods can differ. Central banks can intervene directly and openly by being counterparty and showing themselves, or they can intervene indirectly. Similarly, central bank interventions can be announced in advance in terms of time and amount, or can be carried out without any announcement. The situations requiring intervention take shape within the framework of the monetary policy aims being implemented.

Central Government Primary Budget Balance Central Government Primary Budget Balance

Risks such as the reduction of the value of foreign-currency liabilities by the alteration of exchange rate regulations by the central bank or competent authority of the country invested in, or the non-realization of direct debt or interest payments, constitute sovereign risk.

The settlement of the said transactions realized on Borsa Istanbul is completed when the transfer of the securities in the investors' accounts at MKK takes place, in line with the customer-based netted transaction results received from Takasbank. Within this scope, for the untroubled realization of the clearing and settlement of the purchase and sale transactions of the said capital market instruments traded on Borsa Istanbul, the MKK system must operate in coordination with Takasbank's system.

Consumer Price Index - CPI Consumer Price Index – CPI

An index measuring changes in the prices of goods and services purchased by consumers. In calculating the CPI, first the expenditures made over a year on goods and services by a sample population representing the country as a whole are compiled. According to this expenditure structure, the relative weights of goods and services within the index are determined; goods and services consumed in large amounts by the sample population carry higher weights, while less-consumed ones carry lower weights. The prices of the determined goods and services are collected from all provinces on specific days of each month. The CPI is calculated using the collected prices and the weights set at the start of the year.

Consumer surplus consumer surplus

It is the difference between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay for a product and the lower of its prices on the market.

Consumer Tendency Survey Consumer Trends Survey

It is a survey conducted monthly to measure consumers' personal financial situations, their assessment of the current state of the general economy, their expectations for the coming period and their spending and saving tendencies in the near future.

Consumer Welfare

Refers to the individual benefits consumers obtain from the consumption of goods and services. Individual welfare is defined theoretically as the individual assessments individuals make about their own satisfaction. Therefore, for consumer welfare to be measured exactly, individual preferences must be known exactly. In practice, the concept of consumer surplus is used to measure consumer welfare. Total consumer welfare is the consumer surplus for all consumers

Core Inflation Core Inflation

The permanent component of inflation stripped of temporary or one-off effects. Excluding certain items from the index, statistical analysis and model-based methods are frequently used in calculating core inflation; however, the most widely followed core inflation indicators are those obtained by the exclusion method. For this purpose, energy prices, basic food prices and indirect taxes, which are more exposed to external effects (energy prices, seasonal conditions, fiscal policy, etc.) and can be temporary in nature, are the main elements excluded from such inflation calculations. The purpose of using core inflation is to identify the elements that make the change in the general price level persistent. Although the inflation target is set on headline CPI inflation, the course of core inflation indicators is also taken into account so that more realistic policy decisions can be taken. In particular, in countries moving toward inflation targeting, rather than a single core inflation indicator, alternative indices calculated in different ways are followed, considering the advantages and disadvantages of all calculation methods.

Core Liabilities Core Liabilities

The deposit-based funding that the banking sector obtains from savholders domestically is generally called core liabilities. Since core liabilities grow in proportion to the economy's size and the household's wealth, they are accepted as a source compatible with the economy's fundamentals for banks' credit expansion. In the credit expansion periods in which credits grow faster than deposits, banks can turn to non-core liabilities and thereby feed the macro-financial vulnerabilities in the financial system.

According to this principle, the institutions authorized in banking supervision should ensure that banks have sufficient and appropriate policies, including the 'know your customer' principle, that secure high ethical standards in the financial sector and prevent banks from being misused by criminals

cost-benefit analysis cost-benefit analysis

cost-push inflation cost-push inflation

Country Risk Country Risk

Refers to the probability of loss to which any bank or institution holding, in its portfolio, the direct and/or indirect risk of the debtors in a foreign country is exposed, as a result of the debtors in that country (central government, corporate or other) failing to fulfill, or avoiding, their external obligations owing to the events and uncertainties affecting the economic, social and political conditions. Besides the deteriorated economic, political and social conditions increasing the probability of default in a country; events such as expropriation, moratorium, repudiation of debt, devaluation, and the lack of transparency in the practices of the country's supervisory authority also fall within the scope of country risk. Country risk fundamentally covers sovereign risk, transfer risk and contagion risk, and also includes the categories of indirect exchange rate risk, macroeconomic risk and indirect country risk.

Cross Rate Cross Rate

In the international markets, since exchange rates are generally expressed as the rate of exchange between the US dollar and the local currencies, the rate of exchange between two currencies other than the US dollar can be calculated indirectly according to their prices in US dollars. The cross rate is the exchange rate defined as the parity between two foreign currencies and each of these two currencies with a third foreign currency (usually the US dollar). In other words, the indirect rate of exchange between two currency units is called the cross rate.

Despite the pro-competitive aspects of such an agreement structure, such as the reduction of transaction costs and the bringing together of complementary technologies, in certain cases they can have restrictive effects on competition.

Dollarization Dollarisation / Currency Substitution

The use by residents of a country of foreign currency instead of their own national currency as a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value. Full dollarization is a country's completely abandoning its national currency and adopting a foreign currency as its official currency. Partial dollarization arises when economic units in a country, in an environment of high inflation and uncertainty, begin to prefer financial assets in foreign currency instead of financial assets in national currency, in order to protect themselves from the possible loss of value of the national currency. It is generally observed that the first stage of dollarization develops as 'asset substitution', the beginning of the use of foreign-currency assets as a store of value. On the other hand, the banking crises frequently experienced in the recent past, particularly in emerging markets, have added to the economics literature the concept of 'liability dollarization' in addition to asset dollarization. Liability dollarization is the holding of large amounts of foreign-currency liabilities by all economic units in the country, including the banking and public sectors. Both asset and liability dollarization can be gathered under a single heading and characterized as 'financial dollarization'. Financial dollarization is defined as the tendencies of residents of a country to hold both their assets and their liabilities in foreign currency.
